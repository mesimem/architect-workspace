You are not becoming a QA engineer. You are becoming the architect who directs QA engineers and AI coding agents, evaluates the tests and evidence they hand back, and approves or refuses a release on that evidence. The worked example

Harborline Hospitality Group — Project MISE

Every document in this guide is written for one invented but entirely believable restaurant-industry initiative. Concrete beats generic: you cannot judge a test case in the abstract, only against a system that has real stores, real deadlines and a real way of losing money.

The organisation

Harborline Hospitality Group operates 240 fast-casual coastal-grill restaurants across 14 U.S. states. Roughly 62% of cost of goods is fresh protein and produce with a 36–72 hour shelf life. Prep quantities are decided the night before by a Kitchen Manager working from instinct and a printed four-week average. Group-wide food waste ran at 8.4% of food cost last fiscal year — about $19.6M — while 3,100 stockout events cost an estimated $4.2M in walked guests.

The initiative

Project MISE (Managed Inventory & Sales Estimation) is an AI-assisted demand-forecasting and prep-planning platform. Every night it forecasts 14 days of item-level demand for each store, converts tomorrow's forecast into a prep sheet, and delivers that sheet to the store tablet before the 04:30 opening shift. Kitchen Managers may override any line with a reason code; overrides and end-of-day waste feed back into the model.

The discipline

Why QA exists

Quality assurance is not the activity of finding bugs. It is the activity of producing justified confidence — the evidence that lets a named human sign their name to a release and be right. Finding bugs is a by-product.

The one-sentence version

Definition to keep

Testing tells you what the system does. Quality assurance tells you whether what it does is good enough to ship, and leaves behind the evidence that says why. An architect who conflates the two will approve releases on the strength of a green build.

Four things QA actually buys you

What it buysWhy it matters to the architectProject MISE example
Decision evidence A release decision is a bet. Evidence converts a bet into a calculated risk you can defend in a post-incident review. Without it you are relying on the fact that nobody complained yet. "Ship" is signed against 1,681 executed tests, 0 open Critical defects and a measured 16.3% MAPE — not against a green CI badge.
Change confidence A regression suite is what makes the next change cheap. Teams without one slow down permanently, because every change requires re-reasoning about the whole system. The 1,182-test unit suite runs in 3m 40s, so a forecasting-model swap can be attempted on a Tuesday afternoon.
Specification pressure Writing a test forces the requirement to become precise. "The forecast should be accurate" cannot be tested; "item-store-day MAPE ≤ 18% on a 30-day rolling holdout" can. REQ-015 only became measurable when QA refused to accept "accurate" and demanded a metric, a window and a threshold.
Blast-radius containment Most catastrophic incidents are ordinary bugs meeting an untested failure path. QA's job is to walk the failure paths before production does. DEF-0134 (duplicate audit entry on network flap) was found by a chaos-retry harness, not by a user.

What changes when the code is AI-generated

An AI coding agent will happily produce a large, confident, green test suite that proves almost nothing. Three properties of generated suites are worth internalising before you review one:

  • Generated tests inherit generated assumptions. If the agent misunderstood the requirement, it writes a test that asserts the misunderstanding — and the test passes. A passing suite is not evidence that the requirement was met; it is evidence that the code matches the test.
  • Generated suites skew hard to the happy path. Left unprompted, agents produce positive cases at roughly four to one over everything else. Negative, boundary and idempotency coverage is where the defects live, and it is exactly what you have to ask for by name.
  • Generated tests can be tautological. A test that mocks the unit under test, or that asserts calculateTotal() equals calculateTotal(), will pass forever and detect nothing. Coverage percentage will look excellent.
The architect's non-negotiable

You do not approve a release because the tests pass. You approve it because you have satisfied yourself that the tests, had the system been broken, would have failed. Everything in the review lens is a way of checking that one property.

1 · Test strategy vs test plan vs test cases

These three are constantly confused, including by AI agents, which will cheerfully produce a document titled "Test Plan" that is actually a strategy with no dates in it. The distinction is about scope of authority and rate of change.

ArtifactAnswersChangesOwned by / signed byIn this guide
Test StrategyHow do we test anything here?Once or twice a year QA lead writes; Head of Engineering and the architect approve. It binds every project in the portfolio. Document 1
Test PlanHow do we test this release?Every release Test lead writes; product owner and release manager approve. Names dates, environments, people, entry and exit criteria. Document 2
Test CasesWhat exactly do we execute?Continuously Engineers and agents write; QA reviews. Each one is an executable, unambiguous statement of expected behaviour. Document 3
Test for the distinction

If a sentence would still be true on your next project, it belongs in the strategy. If it names a date, an environment or a person, it belongs in the plan. If it can pass or fail, it belongs in a test case. A "strategy" containing a sprint date is a plan wearing the wrong label.

2 · The testing pyramid

The pyramid is a statement about economics, not about virtue. Tests at the bottom are fast, cheap, precise about where the fault is, and stable. Tests at the top are slow, expensive, vague about causes, and flaky — but they are the only ones that prove the whole thing works together. You want many of the former and few of the latter, and you want each test at the lowest tier that can still answer the question you are asking.

The anti-pattern to name out loud

An inverted pyramid — more end-to-end tests than unit tests — is the single most common shape produced when nobody is directing the suite, and it is what an AI agent will build if you ask it to "add tests" for a feature it can see end to end. The symptoms are a suite that takes 40 minutes, fails randomly twice a week, and tells you "checkout is broken" without telling you which of nine services did it. When you see this shape, push assertions down the pyramid before you add a single new test.

3 · Functional vs non-functional testing

Functional testing asks: does it do the right thing? Non-functional testing asks: does it do it well enough — fast enough, safely enough, reliably enough, accessibly enough? Functional failures get found in a demo. Non-functional failures get found in production, at scale, on the worst day of the quarter.

The most common review finding in this guide

A non-functional requirement without a number is decoration. "The system should be responsive", "the forecast should be accurate", "the UI should be accessible" cannot pass or fail, so no test can be written and no defect can be raised. When an AI agent hands you an NFR section that reads like this, your job is to send it back with the metric, the threshold, the load condition and the measurement window filled in.

4 · Given / When / Then acceptance tests

Given/When/Then (Gherkin form) is a discipline for writing a test that a business stakeholder can read and an engineer can implement without asking a follow-up question. Three clauses, each with one job:

GIVEN — the state

Everything that must be true before the action. Precise, and complete enough that two engineers set up the same world. No actions here.

WHEN — the single action

Exactly one triggering event. If you need "and then" in a WHEN, you have two tests, or a scenario that belongs in a different tier.

THEN — the observable outcome

What an outside observer can verify. State, response, side effect, absence of a side effect. Never "the code should call saveForecast()".

TC-015 — Duplicate override submission is idempotent            REQ-004, REQ-016   [IDEMPOTENCY]

GIVEN  store HBL-0142 has an unlocked prep sheet PS-2026-09-05-0142 for business date 2026-09-05
  AND  line item SKU-4471 "Blackened Salmon 6oz" has a forecast quantity of 44 portions
  AND  Kitchen Manager u-8831 is authenticated and assigned to store HBL-0142
 WHEN  the client POSTs an override of 44 -> 52 with reason code MGR_LOCAL_EVENT
        and header Idempotency-Key: 6f2a1c94-3d77-4b0e-9a55-1e2f8c40db31
  AND  the identical request, byte for byte, is POSTed again 900 ms later
 THEN  both responses are HTTP 200 with the same override id OVR-118204
  AND  the line quantity on PS-2026-09-05-0142 is 52, not 60
  AND  exactly one row exists in override_audit for that idempotency key
  AND  the second response carries header Idempotent-Replay: true
What makes that a good acceptance test

It names real identifiers, so it is reproducible. It has one WHEN. Its THEN clauses assert the response, the resulting state, and the absence of a duplicate side effect — the third one is the assertion an AI agent will leave out unless you name it. And it carries its requirement IDs, so the traceability matrix builds itself.

5 · Positive, negative, boundary and idempotency cases

Four categories. A feature covered only by the first is not tested; it is demonstrated.

CategoryWhat it provesProject MISE example
POSITIVEThe documented behaviour works with valid input. Manager overrides 44 → 52 with a valid reason code; the sheet reflects 52. (TC-012)
NEGATIVEInvalid input, missing auth, or an unavailable dependency is rejected cleanly — correct status code, no partial write, no stack trace to the client. Override submitted with no reason code returns 400 with a field-level error and writes nothing. (TC-013)
BOUNDARYBehaviour at the edges: zero, one, maximum, maximum plus one, empty, the last valid value and the first invalid one. Override quantity 0 is accepted (kitchen preps none); −1 is rejected; 9,999 is accepted; 10,000 is rejected. (TC-014)
IDEMPOTENCYDoing it twice equals doing it once. The single most under-tested property in distributed systems and the one an AI agent almost never adds unprompted. The nightly batch re-run for the same business date produces zero duplicate forecast rows. (TC-004)

Boundary values worth asking for by name

  • Numeric: 0, 1, −1, the minimum, the maximum, maximum + 1, and the value that overflows the column type.
  • Collections: empty, exactly one, exactly the page size, page size + 1, the configured maximum (Project MISE: 400 line items on one sheet), and maximum + 1.
  • Strings: empty, whitespace only, maximum length, maximum + 1, Unicode beyond the basic plane, and a right-to-left sequence.
  • Time: midnight, the end of a month, 29 February, the daylight-saving spring-forward hour that does not exist, the autumn hour that happens twice, and the year boundary.
  • Concurrency: the same operation issued twice within one second, and two different actors editing the same record.

6 · Traceability to requirements

Traceability is a two-way mapping between requirements and tests, and it exists to answer two questions that are asked at exactly the worst moment.

Forward: "Is REQ-011 tested?"

A requirement with no test is an untested requirement — a promise nobody checked. In a mature matrix this count is zero, always, and it is the first number an architect should look at.

Backward: "Why does TC-029 exist?"

A test with no requirement is an orphan test. Sometimes it is a genuinely valuable regression guard, in which case the requirement was missing and should be written. More often it is scope somebody added quietly, and it will fail one day for a reason nobody can adjudicate.

The matrix is also how you make an impact assessment cheap: when REQ-004 changes, the matrix tells you in one lookup that TC-012, TC-013, TC-014 and TC-015 must be re-reviewed, plus the override rows of the coverage matrix. See Document 4 for the complete 18-requirement matrix.

Two numbers to demand on every matrix

Untested requirements: 0. Orphan tests: 0. If either is non-zero and the reason is not written down next to it, the matrix is not finished and the release is not ready to assess.

7 · Coverage — and what it does not mean

"Coverage" is four different measurements sharing one word. Conflating them is how a team ends up proudly reporting 92% while shipping an untested requirement.

KindMeasuresMISE R1Honest interpretation
Requirement coverageRequirements with at least one linked test, over total requirements 18/18 — 100%The one that governs the ship decision.
Code coverage (line)Lines executed by the suite, over total lines 87.4% backend
79.2% frontend		Tells you what was run, not what was checked. Useful mainly inverted: the uncovered 12.6% is a list of what nobody looked at.

Coverage is a floor, never a ceiling

100% line coverage is achievable by a suite with no assertions at all — every line runs, nothing is checked. Treat a coverage number as a way of finding what is definitely untested, never as evidence that what is covered is correct. Chasing the last few percent usually buys tests of getters; the same effort spent on a boundary or idempotency case buys a defect.

8 · Defect severity vs priority

Two independent axes that are constantly collapsed into one. Severity is a property of the defect: how badly does it damage the system when it occurs? Priority is a property of the schedule: how soon must we spend engineering time on it? Severity is assessed by QA and is not negotiable. Priority is set by the product owner and is a business call.

9 · Regression and exploratory testing

Regression testing — the ratchet

Re-running existing tests to prove that a change did not break what already worked. Its value compounds: every defect you fix should leave behind a test that fails on the old code and passes on the new, so the same defect can never return. That is the ratchet — quality that cannot slide backwards.

Governing metric: automation rate. Project MISE runs 94.1% of the regression suite unattended; the remaining 5.9% is 22 manual cases, mostly physical tablet and printer behaviour. Below about 80%, regression stops running every build and starts running "when there's time", which is the same as not running.

Exploratory testing — the search

Simultaneous learning, test design and execution by a skilled human with a charter and a time box. It is not ad-hoc clicking. A charter reads: "Explore the override flow on a tablet with a 40% packet-loss network for 90 minutes, to discover duplicate-write and stale-state defects."

It exists because scripted tests can only find the defects someone already imagined. On Project MISE, 28 sessions produced 34 defects, 9 of them S1 or S2 — including DEF-0134, the worked example in Document 5. This is the one thing on this page an AI agent cannot do for you.

10 · Shift-left

Shift-left means moving quality activity earlier, toward the left of the delivery timeline, because the cost of a defect rises by roughly an order of magnitude at every stage it survives. The number is approximate; the shape is not.

What shift-left looks like in practice

  • Test the requirement, not just the code. Read every acceptance criterion and ask "how would I prove this is false?" If you cannot, the criterion is not testable and must be rewritten.
  • Write acceptance tests before implementation. Given/When/Then written at grooming becomes the definition of done, not a retrospective description of whatever got built.
  • Static gates before dynamic ones. Type checking, schema validation, linting and dependency scanning catch whole classes of defect in seconds, at commit time.
  • Contract tests at every seam. On Project MISE the POS payload schema is asserted in CI, so an upstream field rename fails a build instead of quarantining a night of sales data.
  • Shift right too. Synthetic monitoring, canary releases and production alerting are the acknowledgement that some conditions only exist in production. Shift-left is not an excuse to stop watching after deploy.

Good testing vs bad testing

Both produce green builds. Only one produces confidence. These are the twelve discriminators to run over any suite an engineer or an agent hands you.

DimensionGoodBad
Intent Written to find a failure. The author asked "how could this be wrong?" Written to raise the coverage number, or to satisfy a definition-of-done checkbox.
Failure message Names the behaviour: expected exactly 1 audit row for idempotency key, found 2. You can triage without opening the code. AssertionError: expected true to be false. Every failure requires a debugging session.
Determinism Same input, same result, every run, in any order, on any machine. Clock and randomness are injected. Depends on Date.now(), on test execution order, on a shared database left over from the last run, or on a sleep(2000).

Mocking Mocks the boundary — the POS API, the weather provider, the clock. The logic under test is real. Mocks the unit under test, so the test asserts the mock's behaviour. Always green. Detects nothing.

Mutation testing — the honest check on a suite

If you want to know whether a suite would actually catch a bug, deliberately introduce one and see whether anything turns red. Mutation testing automates this: it flips comparison operators, removes statements, and changes constants, then reports what percentage of those mutants the suite killed. Project MISE runs Stryker nightly on the forecast-guardrail and idempotency modules and holds a 78% mutation score gate there. A suite with 90% line coverage and a 30% mutation score is decorative, and this is the cheapest way to find that out.

The discipline

The architect's review lens

You will rarely have time to read 1,681 tests. You do not need to. These four passes, in order, will surface almost every material weakness in an AI-generated test suite in about twenty minutes.

Pass 1 — Shape (2 minutes)

  • What is the tier distribution? If end-to-end outnumbers unit, stop and fix the shape first.
  • How long does the full suite take? Over ~15 minutes for the pre-merge gate and engineers will route around it.
  • Count case types. Sort the suite by name and count how many contain "invalid", "missing", "duplicate", "boundary", "max", "zero", "concurrent", "retry". If the ratio of non-positive to positive cases is under about 1:1, the suite is a demo.

Pass 2 — Traceability (5 minutes)

  • Open the matrix. Untested requirements must be zero. Any non-zero value needs a written reason on the row, approved by you.
  • Orphan tests must be zero. Each one is either a missing requirement or unapproved scope.
  • Spot-check three requirements by reading the tests they claim. Does the test actually verify the requirement, or something adjacent that was easier to write? This is where AI-generated matrices break: the link exists, but the test checks a different thing.

Pass 3 — Depth on the risky paths (10 minutes)

Do not review everything. Pick the three highest-blast-radius behaviours — on Project MISE those are prep sheet publication, the override write path, and POS ingestion — and interrogate only those.

AskWhat a good answer looks like
"Show me the idempotency test."A test that performs the operation twice and asserts a single side effect — one row, one email, one sheet. Not a test that merely calls it twice and checks no exception.
"What happens when the dependency times out?"A named test with an injected timeout, an asserted retry count, an asserted backoff, and an asserted terminal state — fallback, dead-letter, or a specific error class.
"Show me the negative auth test."Unauthenticated returns 401; authenticated-but-wrong-store returns 403 and the response body contains no data from the other store. The second half is the one that gets skipped.
"What are the boundaries here, and where are they tested?"An explicit list — 0, 1, max, max+1, empty, DST — each mapped to a test ID. If the answer is a paragraph rather than a list of IDs, they are not tested.
"If I broke this deliberately, which test goes red?"A specific test name, immediately. Hesitation means the behaviour is covered in name only.

Pass 4 — Evidence (3 minutes)

  • Are non-functional results measured numbers against stated thresholds, or adjectives?
  • Does every open defect carry both severity and priority, set independently?
  • Does the summary report end in an explicit ship or no-ship recommendation, with named conditions, named risk owners and a rollback trigger?
  • Is there a known-issues list that the support organisation has actually seen?
Four failure patterns specific to AI-generated suites

1. The tautology. The test mocks the thing it is testing. Look for a mock whose name matches the unit under test.
2. The happy-path monoculture. Forty tests, thirty-eight positive. Fixed by naming the four case types in the prompt.
3. The plausible mismatch. The test is well written and asserts the wrong requirement — usually a subtly different reading of an ambiguous acceptance criterion. Only spot-checking finds this.
4. The invented API. The test calls a helper the agent assumed exists, then a second agent creates the helper to make it pass. The test now describes fiction that the codebase has been bent to match.

1Purpose and scope

This strategy defines how Harborline tests. It is deliberately independent of any single release: it states the quality objectives every product must meet, the levels and types of testing that must exist, the standing entry and exit criteria, the defect taxonomy, and the division of responsibility between engineers, quality engineers, AI coding agents and the solution architect.

It applies to all software that reaches a Harborline restaurant, guest or employee, including back-office services, store tablet applications, scheduled batch jobs and machine-learning components. Per-release specifics — dates, environments, named staff, feature scope — belong in a Test Plan derived from this strategy. The Release 1 plan for Project MISE is HHG-QA-PLN-R1-004.

Testing exists to produce the evidence on which a named person approves a release. Any activity that does not change what that person would decide is, at best, a lower priority. Any release decision made without that evidence is a guess wearing a process.

6Testing probabilistic and AI-assisted components

A forecasting model cannot be asserted the way a total can. Harborline therefore tests probabilistic components on five separable properties, four of which are fully deterministic and belong in ordinary tests.

PropertyDeterministic?How it is tested
AccuracyNOStatistical gate against a frozen holdout set, expressed as a metric, a threshold, a window and a per-segment floor. Never a single aggregate number: an 18% group MAPE can hide a store at 60%.
GuardrailsYESFeed the guardrail synthetic model outputs directly. The guardrail is ordinary code with ordinary boundaries and must be unit-tested at, just below and just above every threshold.

10.1 Use of AI coding agents in test creation

AI agents may draft test cases, implement tests and generate test data. They may not approve a suite, set a defect severity or author a Test Summary Report. Agent-drafted tests are subject to four mandatory human checks before merge, performed by a named reviewer:

1Purpose and how to read a case

Each case below is an acceptance test: a statement of behaviour precise enough that two engineers who have never spoken would build the same check, and that a Kitchen Manager could confirm is the behaviour they want. Every case carries a requirement identifier, a type, an execution level, a priority and its result on the release candidate.

FieldMeaning

WHENExactly one triggering event. A case needing two WHENs is two cases — except where the second is a deliberate repeat, which is what makes an idempotency case an idempotency case.
THENExternally observable outcomes: response, resulting state, side effects, and where it matters, the side effects that must not exist.
TypePositive, Negative, Boundary, Idempotency or Non-functional.
LevelUnit, Integration, E2E, Performance, Security, Accessibility, Model or Chaos.

1When to raise a defect

Raise a defect whenever observed behaviour differs from specified behaviour, or when specified behaviour is absent and should not be. Raise it even when you intend to fix it yourself in the next ten minutes: the log is the release evidence, not a work queue. Do not raise a defect for a change of scope — that is a requirement change and follows a different path.

The one-line test of a well-written defect

An engineer who has never seen the feature can reproduce it on the first attempt, from the report alone, without asking a question. If they cannot, the report is not finished — and the time you saved writing it will be spent three times over in the triage meeting.

Per REQ-016, repeated delivery of the same logical operation produces exactly one side effect. The query returns one row: OVR-118204, before 44, after 52. The retry returns HTTP 200 with the same override id and the header Idempotent-Replay: true.

4.3 Actual result

The query returns two rows. The second row records before 52, after 52 — a phantom edit that never happened, attributed to a real employee at a real timestamp. The line quantity on the sheet is correct at 52, so the kitchen is unaffected; the compliance export for the period is not.

4.4 Impact and workaround

  • Business impact: the compliance export overstates override activity. At the observed store-network flap rate of roughly 0.4% of override submissions, projected to 240 stores this is on the order of 60–90 phantom rows per month — enough to fail a sampling audit.
  • Guest impact: none. The prep quantity is correct; the kitchen preps 52, not 60.
  • Workaround: phantom rows are identifiable because before_value = after_value and can be filtered from the export. This is a reporting workaround, not a fix — the underlying record is still wrong, and filtering by that predicate would also hide a legitimate no-op edit.

4.5 Root cause

The service claimed the idempotency key after committing the audit insert, in a second transaction:

// pre-fix — overrideService.ts
const existing = await idem.find(key);          // 1. lookup
if (existing) return replay(existing);
const audit = await auditStore.insert(payload); // 2. side effect committed
await idem.claim(key, audit.id);                // 3. key written — too late
return ok(audit);

Between step 2 and step 3 there is a window — measured at 6 to 40 ms — in which the side effect exists and the key does not. A retry arriving in that window, or after a client timeout that expired while step 2 was in flight, finds no key and proceeds as new work. The idempotency_keys table also carried no unique constraint, so nothing at the storage layer could catch the second claim. Two defects, one mechanism: ordering, and a missing constraint that would have converted the ordering bug into a caught error.

4.6 Fix

Pull request #1,884, merged 2026-08-24 into RC2.

// post-fix — overrideService.ts
return db.transaction(async (tx) => {
  const claimed = await idem.claim(tx, key);      // UNIQUE(key); throws on conflict
  if (!claimed) return replay(await idem.find(tx, key));
  const audit = await auditStore.insert(tx, payload);
  await idem.attach(tx, key, audit.id);
  return ok(audit);                                // key and side effect commit together
});
  • Added UNIQUE(idempotency_key) to idempotency_keys, migration 0042.
  • Key claim and side effect now commit in a single transaction, so the window is closed by construction rather than narrowed.
  • Conflict on claim returns the original resource with Idempotent-Replay: true rather than an error.
  • The same pattern was applied to the other ten write endpoints in the same pull request, since the mechanism was not specific to overrides.

Practice

Directing AI to write tests

The difference between a suite that proves something and a suite that decorates a pull request is almost entirely in the instruction. These are the prompts, and the reasons each clause is there.

The shape of a good instruction

Six elements. Omit any one and you will get the default behaviour, which is thirty positive cases.

  1. The requirement, quoted. Not a paraphrase. The agent will test what you write, so write what the requirement says.
  2. The case types you require, named. Positive, negative, boundary, idempotency. Naming them is what moves the ratio from 1:4 to better than 1:1.
  3. The boundaries, enumerated. You know the domain; the agent does not. "Zero, one, the maximum, maximum plus one, empty, and the DST spring-forward hour" is a specification.
  4. The tier. Say where each assertion belongs, or you will get end-to-end tests for arithmetic.
  5. The prohibitions. No mocking the unit under test. No sleep. No reading the system clock. No asserting on private methods.
  6. The evidence you want back. A traceability table, and a statement of which test would fail if the behaviour broke.

Prompt 1 — generate acceptance cases from a requirement

Write acceptance test cases in Given/When/Then for this requirement, exactly as stated:

  REQ-004: "A Kitchen Manager shall be able to override the quantity of any line on their own
  store's unlocked prep sheet, supplying a reason code drawn from the controlled list."

Produce cases in ALL FOUR categories, and label each one:
  POSITIVE     - the documented behaviour with valid input
  NEGATIVE     - missing reason code, invalid reason code, wrong store, no auth, locked sheet
  BOUNDARY     - quantity -1, 0, 1, the configured maximum, maximum + 1
  IDEMPOTENCY  - the same request delivered twice with the same Idempotency-Key

Rules:
  - One WHEN per case. If you need "and then", split it.
  - Every THEN asserts the response, the resulting state, AND the side effects that must NOT exist.
  - Use real identifiers: store HBL-0142, sheet PS-2026-09-05-0142, item SKU-4471, user u-8831.
  - State the tier for each case: unit, integration or end-to-end. Put each assertion at the
    lowest tier that can answer it.
  - Do not invent behaviour the requirement does not state. Where the requirement is ambiguous,
    list the ambiguity instead of resolving it silently.

Return a table with: ID, type, tier, priority, Given, When, Then, and the requirement ID.

Prompt 2 — implement the tests

Implement TC-012 through TC-015 against the existing override API.

Constraints:
  - Do NOT mock OverrideService, AuditStore or IdempotencyStore. Mock only the HTTP boundary
    to the POS and weather providers.
  - Inject the clock. No test may read Date.now() directly.
  - No sleep, no polling, no retry. A test that needs a retry is a defect in the test.
  - Each test creates and removes its own data. Tests must pass when run alone and in any order.
  - Failure messages must name the behaviour, not the value:
      good: "expected exactly 1 audit row for idempotency key, found 2"
      bad:  "expected 1 to be 2"

Then tell me, for each test, exactly what change to the production code would make it fail.
If you cannot name one, the test is not testing anything and you should say so.

Prompt 3 — audit a suite you did not write

Audit the test suite in src/services/override/ and report, as a table:

  1. Count by tier (unit / integration / e2e) and the ratio between them.
  2. Count by case type. Report the non-positive : positive ratio explicitly.
  3. Every test that mocks the unit under test, or whose assertion is trivially true.
  4. Every test that reads the system clock, depends on execution order, or uses a fixed sleep.
  5. Every requirement in docs/requirements.md with no linked test  (forward gap).
  6. Every test that names no requirement                            (orphan).
  7. For each of the three highest-risk behaviours, name the single test that would go red
     if that behaviour broke. Where no such test exists, say so plainly.

Do not fix anything. Report only. I will decide what to change.
Why prompt 3 says "do not fix anything"

An agent asked to audit and fix in one pass will tend to fix the easy findings and quietly drop the hard ones from the report, because the report is written last. Separating the passes keeps the finding list honest, and the finding list is the artefact you are actually after.

Prompt 4 — harden after a defect

Defect DEF-0134: a retried override with the same Idempotency-Key created a second
override_audit row, because the key is claimed after the audit insert rather than inside
the same transaction.

Before proposing any fix:
  1. Write a failing test that reproduces this on the CURRENT build. Show me it fails.
  2. List every other write path in this service with the same ordering (claim after side
     effect). Do not fix them yet - list them.

Then propose the fix, and tell me which of the tests already in the suite would have caught
this if it had existed, and why it did not.

Four review questions that catch most of what goes wrong

Ask the agentWhat you are actually checking
"What would I have to break for this test to fail?" Tautology and mocking-the-unit-under-test. A vague answer means the test asserts nothing.
"Which requirement does this test verify, and quote it." The plausible mismatch — a well-written test asserting a subtly different reading of the requirement.
"List the boundaries of this input, then show me the test for each." Boundary coverage. The list is almost always longer than the tests.
"What happens if this operation is delivered twice?" Idempotency. If the answer is "it would create two", you have found a defect before it shipped.
Practice

FAQ

The questions that come up in review meetings, answered the way an architect should answer them. These are also the answers the Ask the Guide assistant draws on.

How much testing is enough?

Enough is when the exit criteria are met, not when a percentage is reached. Concretely: every requirement has a passing test, every Band A capability has all four case types, every non-functional threshold has a measured number, zero Critical defects are open, and every remaining gap is named with a control and an owner. If you can say all five of those sentences and support each with an artefact, you have tested enough for this release. If you are still adding tests because the coverage number looks low, you are optimising the wrong thing.

Can I trust AI-generated tests?

You can trust them the way you trust a fast, confident junior engineer who has not met your users: excellent at volume and structure, unreliable about intent. Four specific failure patterns recur — the tautology (mocks the unit under test), the happy-path monoculture, the plausible mismatch (well-written test, wrong requirement), and the invented API. The first, second and fourth are catchable mechanically. The third needs a human to read three tests against three requirements. Budget twenty minutes for the four-pass review lens and you will catch nearly all of it.

What is the difference between severity and priority?

Severity is technical impact and is assessed by QA from what was observed. Priority is schedule urgency and is set by the Product Owner from business context. They are independent: DEF-0119 is S1/P3 (a genuine crash that nothing can currently reach), DEF-0141 is S4/P1 (a cosmetic truncation that prints in 240 stores before Monday's brand review). If your log shows the two columns matching on nearly every row, one of them is being copied rather than thought about.

What is idempotency and why does QA care so much about it?

An operation is idempotent when performing it twice leaves the same end state as performing it once. It matters because networks lose acknowledgements, clients retry, queues deliver at least once, and operators re-run jobs. Every one of those is normal, and every one of them will deliver your write operation twice. If the second delivery creates a second row, a second email or a second charge, the system is broken — not fragile. It is the most under-tested property in distributed systems and the one an AI agent almost never adds unless you name it. See TC-004, TC-011, TC-015, TC-020 and TC-035.

How do you test something probabilistic, like a forecasting model?

Split it into five properties, four of which are ordinary deterministic code. Accuracy is statistical: gate it with a metric, a threshold, a frozen holdout and a per-segment floor, because an 18% aggregate can hide a single store at 60%. Guardrails are ordinary code with ordinary boundaries — unit-test them at, just below and just above the threshold. Fallback is ordinary code — force a timeout, an error and malformed output, and assert the deterministic substitute. Pipeline determinism means the same inputs and seed produce identical output, without which no failure is reproducible. Explainability payload is a schema contract. Only the first is genuinely statistical; the other four are why a probabilistic system is still testable.

My suite is mostly end-to-end tests. How bad is that?

Bad in a way that compounds. End-to-end tests are slow, flaky and vague about causes: they tell you "checkout is broken" without telling you which of nine services did it. An inverted pyramid produces a suite that takes forty minutes, fails randomly twice a week, and trains the team to re-run rather than investigate. The fix is not to delete end-to-end tests but to push assertions down: for each end-to-end case, ask which single assertion it is really making and whether a unit test could make it in three milliseconds. Harborline's standing rule is that once end-to-end exceeds 15% of the suite, no new end-to-end case may be added until assertions have been moved down.

Should we aim for 100% code coverage?

No. Line coverage is reachable at 100% by a suite with no assertions whatsoever — every line runs, nothing is checked. Its value is diagnostic and inverted: the uncovered fraction is a list of code nobody exercised, and that list is worth reading. Chasing the last few percent buys tests of getters; the same effort spent on one boundary or idempotency case buys a defect. If you want to know whether your suite would actually catch a bug, run mutation testing instead — it introduces deliberate faults and reports how many the suite killed.

Why do I need a traceability matrix if I already have tests?

Because two questions get asked at the worst possible moment and you need a one-lookup answer to both. "Is REQ-011 tested?" — a requirement with no test is a promise nobody checked. "Why does TC-029 exist?" — a test with no requirement is either a missing requirement or unapproved scope. The matrix also makes impact assessment cheap: when a requirement changes, it tells you in one lookup which cases must be re-reviewed in the same change rather than in a follow-up nobody schedules. The two numbers to demand are untested requirements: 0, and orphan tests: 0.

What does "shift-left" actually mean in practice?

Moving quality activity earlier, because the cost of a defect rises roughly tenfold at each stage it survives. In practice: review requirements for testability before any code exists; write acceptance criteria in Given/When/Then at grooming so they become the definition of done; run static gates (types, schemas, lint, secret scan) at commit; assert contracts at every external seam so an upstream rename fails a build instead of a night of data. On Project MISE the single highest-return activity of the release was the review that turned "the forecast should be accurate" into a measurable MAPE threshold — and it happened before implementation started.

With this much automation, is exploratory testing still worth it?

It is the highest-value hour in the schedule. Scripted tests can only find defects somebody already imagined; exploratory testing finds the ones nobody did. It is not ad-hoc clicking — it is charter-based and time-boxed: "explore the override flow on a tablet with 40% packet loss for 90 minutes, to discover duplicate-write and stale-state defects." On Project MISE, 28 such sessions produced 34 defects, 9 of them Critical or High, including DEF-0134, the most serious defect of the release. This is also the one activity on this page that an AI agent cannot do for you.

A test fails about one run in twenty. What should we do?

Treat it as a defect, not a nuisance. A flaky test is either telling you about a real race condition or telling you the test is badly written — both are worth knowing and both are fixable. The dangerous outcome is neither: the team learns to re-run the pipeline, and within a quarter nobody believes any red build, at which point the suite's value is zero. The standing practice is to quarantine the test immediately so it stops blocking, assign an owner, and root-cause it within the sprint. Fix it or delete it; do not leave it quarantined indefinitely. Harborline gates flake rate below 1%; Release 1 finished at 1.4% with nine tests quarantined and owned.

How do I actually make the ship / no-ship call?

Against written criteria decided before you knew the result — that is the whole point of exit criteria. Walk each clause and state a position: met, met with waiver, or not met. Zero open Critical defects has no waiver path. Every waiver must name an impact, a mitigation, an owner and a target release. Then state the recommendation explicitly, in one word, followed by the conditions and the rollback triggers, and say what would have made it a no-ship. A recommendation that reads "generally positive, some risks remain" is not a decision and cannot be audited. See Document 6, section 1.

How do I stop non-functional requirements from being vague?

Refuse the adjective and demand four things: a metric, a threshold, a load or measurement condition, and a window. "The system should be responsive" becomes "GET /v1/prep-sheets responds with p95 ≤ 400 ms and p99 ≤ 900 ms at 300 rps sustained for 15 minutes, error rate below 0.1%." "The forecast should be accurate" becomes "item-store-day MAPE ≤ 18.0% on a frozen 30-day holdout, no store above 30.0%." Until an NFR has those four parts, no test can be written and no defect can be raised against it, which means in practice it is not a requirement at all.

What is regression testing and how much should be automated?

Re-running existing tests to prove a change did not break what already worked. Its value compounds through one rule: every defect you fix leaves behind a test that fails on the pre-fix build and passes after. That is a ratchet — quality that cannot slide backwards. The governing metric is automation rate, and the practical floor is about 90%. Below roughly 80%, regression stops running every build and starts running "when there's time", which is operationally the same as not running. Project MISE finished at 94.1%, with 22 manual cases covering physical tablet and printer behaviour.

I have twenty minutes and 1,681 tests. What do I look at?

Four passes. Shape (2 min): tier distribution, total runtime, and the count of tests whose names contain "invalid", "missing", "duplicate", "boundary", "max", "zero", "concurrent" or "retry". Traceability (5 min): untested requirements must be zero, orphan tests must be zero, then spot-check three requirements against the tests that claim them. Depth (10 min): pick only the three highest-blast-radius behaviours and ask for the idempotency test, the timeout test, the negative auth test, the boundary list, and "if I broke this, which test goes red?" Evidence (3 min): are non-functional results numbers or adjectives; does every defect carry both severity and priority; does the summary end in an explicit recommendation.

What is the difference between a test strategy and a test plan?

Scope of authority and rate of change. A strategy answers "how do we test anything here?", changes once or twice a year, binds every project, and contains no dates. A plan answers "how do we test this release?", changes every release, and names dates, environments, people, entry criteria and exit criteria. A test case answers "what exactly do we execute?" and can pass or fail. The quick test: if a sentence would still be true on your next project it belongs in the strategy; if it names a date or a person it belongs in the plan; if it can pass or fail it belongs in a case.

Glossary

Thirty-two terms an architect will hear in a QA review, defined the way they are used in practice rather than the way they are defined in a standard.

TermDefinition
Acceptance testA test written from the requirement's point of view, usually in Given/When/Then, that a business stakeholder can read and confirm.
Blast radiusHow much of the system, and how many users, a failure in this component can affect. The primary input to how much testing a feature deserves.
BlockedA test that could not be executed for an external reason. It is not a pass, and reporting it as one is how a release decision becomes fiction.
Boundary valueAn input at the edge of a valid range. Defects cluster here because edges are where the off-by-one lives.
Branch coverageThe proportion of conditional outcomes exercised, both true and false. Strictly more honest than line coverage.
Canary releaseDeploying to a small subset of production first, with defined success criteria and an armed rollback, before proceeding.
Chaos testingDeliberately injecting infrastructure failure — latency, packet loss, duplicate delivery, connection reset — to observe behaviour under conditions production will eventually supply.
CharterThe one-sentence mission of an exploratory session: what to explore, with what resources, to discover what kind of information.
Contract testA test asserting that a producer and consumer agree on a schema, run in both builds so a breaking change fails CI rather than production.
Correlation IDA unique identifier generated at the entry point of a request or job and propagated through every log line and downstream call, so a symptom can be traced to a cause.
Defect escape rateDefects found in production as a share of all defects found for that release. The only metric that directly measures whether QA is working.
DeterminismSame inputs, same result, every run, in any order, on any machine. Without it, no failure is reproducible and no defect is triageable.
Entry criteriaConditions that must hold before a test phase begins. Skipping them produces a cycle that measures the environment rather than the software.
Exit criteriaConditions that must hold before a release is approved. Written before results are known, which is what makes them criteria rather than rationalisations.
Exploratory testingSimultaneous learning, test design and execution by a skilled human working to a charter and a time box. Finds the defects nobody imagined.
Flaky testA test that passes and fails on unchanged code. A defect in its own right; above about 2% of the suite it destroys trust in every red build.
GherkinThe Given/When/Then notation. A discipline for precision, not a tool requirement.
Holdout setData frozen and deliberately withheld from model training, used to measure accuracy honestly. Contaminating it invalidates every accuracy claim made against it.
IdempotencyThe property that performing an operation twice leaves the same end state as performing it once.
Idempotency keyA client-supplied identifier for a logical operation, stored server-side, so that a retry is recognised as a replay rather than as new work.
Integration testA test exercising real internal seams — service to database, service to queue — with external providers stubbed against a recorded contract.
MAPEMean absolute percentage error. The standard accuracy metric for demand forecasting. Must always be paired with a per-segment floor, since an aggregate can hide a single failing segment.
Mutation testingDeliberately introducing faults into the code and measuring how many the suite catches. The honest check on whether high coverage means anything.
Negative testA test that invalid input, missing authorisation or an unavailable dependency is rejected cleanly — correct status, no partial write, no internal detail leaked.
Orphan testA test that traces to no requirement. Either the requirement is missing or the scope was never approved. Both need resolving; neither is harmless.
PriorityHow soon a defect must be fixed. A business judgement, set by the Product Owner, independent of severity.
Regression testA test re-run to prove a change did not break existing behaviour. Every fixed defect should leave one behind.
Requirement coverageThe proportion of requirements with at least one linked, executed, passing test. The coverage that governs the ship decision.
Risk coverageThe proportion of entries on the risk register with a mitigating test. The coverage executives care about, and the one most often absent.
SeverityHow badly a defect damages the system when it occurs. A technical judgement, assessed by QA, not negotiable in triage without new evidence.
Shift-leftMoving quality activity earlier in the delivery timeline, because a defect costs roughly ten times more at each stage it survives.
Traceability matrixThe bidirectional mapping between requirements and tests. Answers "is this tested?" and "why does this test exist?" in one lookup each.
