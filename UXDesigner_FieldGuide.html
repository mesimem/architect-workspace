UX Designer Field Guide

You are becoming an AI Solution Architect, not a UX designer. Your job is to direct user-experience work, evaluate what comes back from an AI or a human designer, and approve it with your name attached. That takes roughly twenty percent of the discipline — but it has to be the right twenty percent, held with real confidence. This guide is that twenty percent, plus a complete set of nine finished deliverables you can copy, adapt, and re-use.

The one thing to take away

UX is not decoration and it is not taste. It is a set of falsifiable claims about how a specific person will complete a specific task under specific conditions. Every artifact in this guide exists to make one of those claims checkable by someone other than the person who made it. When you review AI-generated UX, you are not asking “do I like this?” — you are asking “which claim does this make, and how would we know if it were wrong?”

Foundations 01

Why UX exists

Software does not fail because it lacks features. It fails because a real person, under time pressure, with imperfect information, could not get the thing done — and quietly went back to the spreadsheet. UX is the discipline of removing that failure mode before you have paid to build it.

The economic argument, stated plainly

Three facts drive every UX budget conversation you will ever have.

  1. Change is cheap early and expensive late. Moving a button on a wireframe costs minutes. Moving it after the API contract, the analytics events, the training materials, and the compliance sign-off all assume its position costs weeks. UX front-loads the cheap decisions.
  2. Unused software has negative value. It carries hosting cost, security surface, support load, and the opportunity cost of the team that built it. A feature at 8% adoption is worse than no feature, because it also fragments the truth — now some data lives in the tool and some in the spreadsheet.
  3. For AI systems specifically, the interface is the trust boundary. A forecast model at 89% accuracy is worthless if the interface gives the user no way to see why it said what it said, no way to override it, and no way to tell a confident prediction from a guess. Users do not reject models. They reject interfaces that ask for blind faith.

Where AI projects die

In the accelerator you will build systems that make recommendations. The recurring failure is not model quality — it is an interface that hides uncertainty. A number rendered in the same weight, colour, and confidence as a measured fact will be read as a measured fact. When it is wrong once, the user distrusts every number the system produces afterwards. Designing how confidence, provenance, and override are shown is a UX decision with direct model-adoption consequences, and it is yours to approve.

The shape of the work: two diamonds

The canonical model is the Design Council's Double Diamond. It matters to you because it tells you which artifact to ask for at which moment — and because most bad AI-generated UX work is generated at the wrong point in the diamond. Asking for mockups during Discover produces confident-looking guesses.

The initiative

Harborline’s kitchen managers decide every morning how much of each of 38 prep items to make: how many pounds of blackened salmon, how many quarts of citrus slaw, how many portions of pickled shallot. Today they decide from a laminated par-sheet, last Tuesday’s memory, and a look out the window at the weather. Over-prep goes in the bin at close. Under-prep means the 12:40 p.m. rush sells out of the highest-margin bowl.

PrepSense is an AI forecasting service that predicts demand per item per store per daypart and turns that into a recommended prep quantity, delivered to a wall-mounted tablet in the back of house at 5:00 a.m. The model is not the hard part. The hard part is that a manager with eleven minutes and wet gloves must be able to read a recommendation, judge whether to trust it, adjust the two items she knows are wrong, and commit — without a trainer standing next to her, because annual turnover in this role is 118%.

Initiative charter — the one paragraph everything traces back to

Reduce food waste from 6.8% to 4.9% of food cost across 74 stores within two quarters of rollout, by giving kitchen managers a prep recommendation they understand well enough to accept or correct in under four and a half minutes, on a shared back-of-house tablet, with no formal training, at 5:40 a.m. Success is measured by waste percentage, unmoderated prep-board task success rate, time on task, and a healthy override rate — not by model accuracy alone.

Personas & jobs-to-be-done

A persona is not a description of a person. It is a decision-making instrument: a compressed model of goals, context, and constraints, precise enough that two people arguing about a design can resolve the argument by consulting it.

The test of a real persona

Ask this: “Could this persona settle a design argument?” If the answer is no, it is a stock photo with a name.

Fails the test

“Marisol, 34, Charleston SC. Married, two kids. Enjoys hiking and true-crime podcasts. Tech-savvy millennial. Uses an iPhone. Motivated and hard-working.”

Nothing here decides anything. Does she want density or whitespace? Does she trust the model? What breaks her morning? Demographics are the most common failure in AI-generated personas because they are the easiest thing to generate.

Passes the test

“Marisol will accept a recommendation she disagrees with rather than spend 90 seconds finding the override, because at 5:47 a.m. the delivery truck matters more. Her goal is a clean 2 p.m. line, not accurate forecasting.”

This decides things. It says the override must be reachable in one tap from the number itself, and it says success metrics must not treat “accepted” as “agreed.” That single sentence generated two design rules and one instrumentation requirement.

Jobs-to-be-Done: the format that keeps you honest

Clayton Christensen’s framing — people do not want a product, they want progress in a circumstance. The job story format forces circumstance and motivation into the same sentence, which is exactly what a feature request leaves out.

Job story template

When [situation, with enough detail to be uncomfortable], I want to [motivation, stated as a verb], so I can [expected outcome that matters to me, not to the business].

Weak: “As a kitchen manager, I want to see forecasts so I can prep correctly.” — circumstance-free, unfalsifiable, generates no design.

Strong: “When it is 5:41 a.m., the produce truck is late, and I can see the forecast wants 22% more slaw than last Thursday, I want to know in one glance what changed so I can decide whether to trust it before my two openers finish clocking in.” — this produces a requirement: the delta versus the same weekday last week, and the top driver of the change, must be on the primary screen, not one level down.

Five checks before you approve a persona set

  1. Is one persona marked primary? If everyone is primary, nobody is, and the design will average into mush. Exactly one persona should get the “design for her first” label.
  2. Are goals separable from tasks? “Log waste” is a task the system imposes. “Not get chewed out in the Monday call” is a goal she brought with her. Designs that serve only imposed tasks get abandoned.
  3. Are frustrations specific and attributable? “Hates slow software” is filler. “The current par-sheet PDF takes 40 seconds to load on store Wi-Fi, so she photographs it once a week and works from the photo” is a finding — and it tells you the real competitor is a camera roll.
  4. Is there a persona who will actively resist? Every rollout has one. Naming them is not pessimism; it is how you design the adoption path. If the set contains only enthusiasts, research was done on volunteers.
  5. Is each persona traceable to evidence? A count of interviews, shifts observed, or tickets read. “Synthesised from 9 interviews and 4 observed opens” is a persona. No provenance line means it was invented — which is fine as a proto-persona, but it must be labelled as one so nobody cites it as fact.

The AI-generated persona failure mode

Ask a model for personas and you will get four well-written, internally consistent, entirely average people. They will have plausible names, tidy goals, and frustrations that could belong to any industry. The tell is interchangeability: swap the job titles and nothing breaks. Real personas are not interchangeable, because real constraints are not. Your correction prompt is: “Rewrite these so that each persona’s frustrations could only belong to this role in this industry, and remove every demographic detail that does not change a design decision.”

User journeys

A journey map is the artifact that stops teams from designing screens in isolation. Its job is to show that the experience is a sequence in time, that emotion moves across that sequence, and that the worst moment is almost never on the screen anyone was arguing about.

The five rows that make a journey map useful

RowWhat it holdsWhy an architect needs it
StagesThe user’s phases in their own words — not your system’s modules. “Walk in and get oriented,” not “Authentication.”Reveals stages your system does not serve at all. Gaps here are missing scope.
ActionsWhat the person physically does, including the things outside your software (opens the walk-in, counts the salmon).Off-screen actions are where integration requirements hide.
ThoughtsVerbatim-style inner speech. “Why is it asking for 22% more slaw?”Every unanswered question in this row is a content or explainability requirement.
EmotionA plotted curve, not adjectives. Anchored at named moments.The trough tells you where to spend budget. Design effort should be proportional to emotional cost, not to screen count.

The unhappy path is the deliverable

Most journey maps you will be shown describe a good day. A user arrives informed, the network works, the data is fresh, and they leave satisfied. That map is nearly worthless, because nobody needed design help with the good day.

Insist on the unhappy path being mapped in the same artifact, at the same fidelity. For PrepSense the unhappy path is not exotic — it happens roughly two mornings a week: the Wi-Fi drops mid-commit, the forecast is stale because last night’s waste log was never submitted, and the recommendation therefore contradicts what the manager can see in the walk-in. Everything about how the system behaves in that ten minutes determines whether it is trusted for the next six months.

Four unhappy-path questions to ask about any journey map

  1. Where does the data go wrong? Stale, missing, contradicted by the physical world. What does the interface say then?
  2. Where does the network go wrong? What is readable offline, what is editable offline, and what happens to an edit made offline when connectivity returns and the server value has changed?
  3. Where does the person go wrong? Wrong store selected, wrong unit entered, tapped commit before adjusting. Is it recoverable, and in how many taps?
  4. Where does the organisation go wrong? The district manager overrides the store, the promo was launched without telling the model, the recipe changed on Tuesday. Who does the interface blame, and is that fair?

Moments of truth

Within any journey, two or three moments carry disproportionate weight. In PrepSense they are precise:

  • First glance (5:41 a.m., ~4 seconds). Does the board look plausible? If the first number she reads is obviously wrong, trust is gone for the whole session and she reverts to the par-sheet.
  • First disagreement (5:44 a.m.). She believes one number is wrong. How the system handles being told it is wrong — gracefully and in one tap, or defensively across three screens — determines whether she ever tells it again. A system that makes disagreement expensive gets silent compliance, which looks like adoption in the metrics and is actually abandonment.
  • Next-day feedback (following 5:41 a.m.). Did yesterday’s override change anything? If the system never acknowledges that she corrected it, she learns her input does not matter. This is the moment almost every AI product forgets to design.

Information architecture

Information architecture is the invisible discipline. Done well, nobody notices. Done badly, every usability problem downstream is blamed on the visual design, and no amount of restyling fixes it.

Three decisions, in order

  1. Grouping. What belongs with what? Group by the user’s task, not by your data model or your org chart. “Waste” and “Prep” live in different database tables and different HQ departments, but for Marisol they are the same loop — what I made yesterday, what I threw away, what I make today — so they belong in one place.
  2. Labelling. What do we call it? Use the words already spoken on the floor. Harborline staff say par, prep list, walk-in, throw-out. They do not say inventory reconciliation, demand signal, or SKU-level yield variance. Every label you invent is a training cost you pay forever.
  3. Structure and depth. How deep, how broad? Shallow and broad beats deep and narrow on touch devices, because tapping is cheap and remembering where you are is expensive.

The three-click guideline — read it correctly

“Three clicks” is not a law about clicks; users will happily click seven times when each click feels like progress. It is a proxy for certainty per step. Use it as a review heuristic in this form: every core task must be reachable in three taps or fewer, and at each tap the user must be able to predict what happens next. For PrepSense, all four core tasks are inside three taps — and the primary one is inside zero, because the prep board is the landing screen.

Breadth versus depth, made concrete

How IA is actually validated

Two cheap methods, both of which you can ask for by name:

  • Card sort (open or closed) — give 8–15 real users the content items on cards and have them group and name the groups. Open sorts reveal their mental model; closed sorts test yours. Output: an agreement matrix showing which items are contested.
  • Tree test — give users a text-only version of the proposed structure and a task (“where would you go to record that you threw out four quarts of slaw?”). Measures success rate, directness, and first-click accuracy without any visual design in the way. A structure that fails a tree test will fail as a product; restyling will not save it.

Wireframe vs mockup vs prototype

These three words are used interchangeably in meetings, and the confusion costs real money. They answer different questions, cost different amounts, and are dangerous when substituted for each other.

Fidelity ladder — what each artifact is for
ArtifactQuestion it answersWhat it deliberately omitsRelative cost to changeDanger when misused
Wireframe

The rule that saves the most money

Never let a decision be made at a fidelity higher than the decision requires. If the argument is “should confidence appear next to the number or in a panel,” that is a wireframe question and must be settled on a wireframe. Settling it on a polished mockup means the team paid 4× for the exploration and will resist changing it, because the polish creates sunk-cost attachment. AI tools make this worse, not better: they generate high-fidelity output instantly, so teams now start at the wrong end of the ladder by default. Your correction is to ask for wireframes explicitly and to refuse colour until structure is agreed.

Annotation is what makes a wireframe a specification

An un-annotated wireframe is a drawing. An annotated wireframe is a contract an engineer can build from and a QA analyst can test against. Every wireframe you approve must carry numbered annotations covering:

Per element

  • What it is, and which component from the design system provides it
  • Where its data comes from, and what happens when that data is absent
  • Interaction: what a tap does, what is disabled and why
  • Content rules: max length, truncation behaviour, units, rounding
  • Target size and spacing, where it differs from the system default

Per screen — the five states

  • Empty — no data yet, or legitimately nothing to show. Must teach, not just apologise
  • Loading — and specifically: what is on screen during 200 ms vs 3 s vs 15 s
  • Partial / stale — some data arrived, some is old. Most-forgotten state in AI products
  • Error — what failed, whether the user caused it, what to do next, and whether work was lost
  • Success — visible, durable confirmation, and a clear next action

Severity ratings — the part that makes the audit actionable

A heuristic evaluation without severity ratings is a list of complaints. With them, it is a prioritised plan. Use Nielsen’s 0–4 scale and require three inputs per finding: frequency (how often it will occur), impact (how hard it is to overcome), and persistence (whether users learn around it or hit it forever).

How to run one in 40 minutes

Three to five evaluators inspect independently (independence is the whole method — group inspection produces one loud person’s opinion), each walking the same task scenarios twice: once for flow, once for detail. Findings are then merged, deduplicated, and severity-rated as a group. Nielsen’s own data says a single evaluator finds roughly 35% of issues and five find about 75%. That is the reason you do not accept a single AI pass as an audit — but three differently-prompted AI passes plus your own review is a genuinely respectable substitute for the first round.

The 20% to know · 08

Accessibility to WCAG 2.1 AA

Accessibility is the one part of UX with a published, testable, legally-referenced standard. That makes it the easiest thing for you to hold a line on, and the least excusable thing to get wrong. It is also where AI-generated UX fails most reliably, because generators optimise for visual appeal and accessibility often costs visual appeal.

POUR — the four principles

P

Perceivable

Information must be available to the senses the user has. Text alternatives, captions, sufficient contrast, content that does not depend on colour, shape, or sound alone.

O

Operable

Every function must be usable by keyboard, by switch, by voice, and by an unsteady hand. No timing traps, no seizure-inducing motion, adequate target sizes, visible focus.

U

Understandable

Text is readable, behaviour is predictable, and input errors are identified in text with instructions for correction. Consistent navigation, consistent identification.

R

Robust

Correct semantic markup so assistive technology can interpret it, and status messages announced programmatically without stealing focus.

Conformance levels — and why AA is the right target

LevelMeaningPractical position
AMinimum. Removes absolute barriers only.Not a defensible target for enterprise software. A-only means keyboard users can technically operate it and nothing more.
AAThe standard referenced by ADA Title II/III settlements, Section 508, EN 301 549, and virtually all enterprise procurement.Your default target. Write it into acceptance criteria. WCAG 2.1 AA adds 12 criteria over 2.0 AA, including reflow, non-text contrast, and orientation — all directly relevant to a mounted tablet.
AAAEnhanced. 7:1 contrast, sign language, extended audio description.Not required wholesale — W3C itself says AAA is not achievable for all content. But selectively adopt AAA criteria where the environment demands it. PrepSense adopts 1.4.6 (7:1 contrast) and 2.5.5 (44px targets, exceeded to 56px) because of glare and gloves.

Contrast: the actual arithmetic

You should be able to sanity-check a contrast claim without a tool. The ratio is (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05) where L is relative luminance, computed per channel as c/255, linearised by ((c+0.055)/1.055)^2.4 above the 0.04045 threshold, then weighted 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B. The green weight dominates — which is why a mid-green and a mid-red of the same apparent brightness give wildly different ratios against white.

Visual & interaction hierarchy

Hierarchy is the answer to one question: if the user looks at this screen for four seconds, what do they come away with? Everything else — alignment, spacing, weight, colour — is a means to that end.

Visual hierarchy: six levers, in order of strength

  1. Position. Top-left in a left-to-right reading culture wins before anything else is processed. You cannot out-style a bad position.
  2. Size. The most reliable signal. A 32px number and a 13px number are not two numbers; they are a headline and a footnote.
  3. Weight and contrast. 700 against 400 at the same size creates a clear tier without adding space — the correct lever on a dense screen.
  4. Colour. Powerful but expensive. Every additional accent colour halves the meaning of the others. Reserve one accent for one meaning: at Colaberry, Cherry Red is the primary action and the key highlight, and nothing else.
  5. Whitespace and proximity. Gestalt proximity is stronger than most designers expect: things near each other are read as related, regardless of borders. Bad grouping cannot be repaired with dividers.
  6. Alignment. Every misalignment is a small question the eye has to answer. A consistent grid removes dozens of those questions per screen.

The one-dominant-element rule

Each screen gets exactly one visually dominant element, and it must be the thing that serves the primary job. On the PrepSense Prep Board that is the recommended quantity per row — not the brand header, not the store selector, not the sync status. If you squint until the screen blurs and the dominant shape is the navigation, the hierarchy is wrong. This is a two-second review technique and it is astonishingly effective on AI-generated layouts, which tend to over-emphasise chrome because chrome is what generators have seen most of.

Interaction hierarchy: three laws worth knowing by name

Fitts’s Law

Time to acquire a target grows with distance and shrinks with target size. Consequence: the most frequent action gets the biggest target in the easiest-to-reach place. On a wall-mounted tablet, “easiest to reach” is the lower-centre band, not the top-right corner where desktop convention puts primary actions.

PrepSense: Commit is a 56px-tall full-width bar pinned to the bottom of the board.

Hick’s Law

Decision time grows logarithmically with the number of equally-weighted choices. Consequence: reduce simultaneous choices, or make them unequal so the eye can discard most instantly. Eleven columns of equal weight is eleven decisions per row, thirty-eight times over.

PrepSense: 4 columns on the row; 34 of 38 rows collapsible behind one “accept unchanged” action.

Progressive disclosure

Show what is needed now; keep the rest one predictable step away. Not hiding — sequencing. The test is whether the user can predict what is behind the step without opening it.

PrepSense: confidence, drivers, 14-day history and unit cost live behind “Why this number?” on each row.

The naming test that catches 90% of bad token sets

Read the token name aloud and ask: “could this value change without the name becoming a lie?” color.red.primary becomes a lie the moment the brand adopts a different accent, so teams keep the old value and drift. action.primary.bg never becomes a lie, because it names the job, not the paint. AI-generated design systems fail this test constantly — they produce blue-600, gray-100, red-500 ladders, which are primitives masquerading as a system. A palette is not a design system.

Type scale — a ratio, not a list of sizes

Pick a ratio and derive. PrepSense uses a 1.200 (minor third) scale from a 16px base, which gives comfortable separation at reading sizes and enough jump at display sizes to establish dominance without shouting. Every size below is a token; no component may invent a size.

Spacing — one base unit, one ladder

Governance — the part that determines whether the system survives

  • One owner. A named person or role approves additions. Systems without an owner become a graveyard of near-duplicate buttons within two quarters.
  • An intake path. A documented way to propose a new component or token, with the criterion “used in three or more places” before it is promoted into the system.
  • Deprecation, not deletion. Mark, warn, migrate, then remove. Silent removal breaks consumers you did not know existed.
  • Contribution audit. Periodically diff production CSS against the token set. Every literal hex or off-ladder pixel value in application code is a system leak with a name and a commit hash attached.

Usability testing

Usability testing is the only activity in this guide that produces evidence rather than opinion. Everything else is a well-reasoned prediction. Five people and ninety minutes will tell you more than five weeks of internal debate — if the tasks are written correctly, which is the part that goes wrong.

Task design: the whole method lives here

A task that teaches you nothing

“Use the Adjust button to change the salmon quantity to 42 pounds, then tap Commit.”

This tests whether the participant can follow instructions. It contains the answer — the interface’s own vocabulary (“Adjust”, “Commit”) is in the prompt, so you learn nothing about whether those labels are discoverable. Every failure will be blamed on the participant.

A task that produces findings

“It’s Thursday morning. A bus tour of forty people cancelled last night. Get today’s prep set up the way you think it should be, and let me know when you’d walk away from the tablet.”

Scenario-framed, goal-stated, path-free, and it uses zero interface vocabulary. “Let me know when you’d walk away” is the completion signal — which also reveals whether the user believes the work is saved, the single most valuable thing this task measures.

Six rules for tasks

  1. Frame a scenario, not a click path. Give the situation and the goal; let the participant find the route.
  2. Never use the interface’s own words. If your task says “Adjust”, you can no longer test whether “Adjust” is the right word.
  3. Define success before the session, in observable terms. “Committed board contains 22–28 qt of slaw and the participant states the board is saved” — not “seemed to get it”.
  4. Include one task that must fail gracefully. Force the unhappy path: turn off the Wi-Fi mid-commit. What the participant does in the next thirty seconds is your most valuable data.
  5. Cap it at five to seven tasks / sixty minutes. Fatigue produces compliance, and compliance looks like success.
  6. Randomise order where tasks are independent. Otherwise you measure learning, not usability.

Sample size — the “five users” number, used correctly

Nielsen and Landauer’s model gives the proportion of problems found as 1 − (1 − L)n; with the typical L = 0.31, five users surface roughly 85% of the discoverable problems in a homogeneous user group. Two caveats that matter more than the number: (1) it is five users per distinct user group — PrepSense needs five kitchen managers and five district managers, because their tasks and contexts do not overlap; (2) it applies to finding qualitative problems, not to measuring anything. If you want a defensible task-success percentage or a comparable SUS score, you need 20–30 participants per group. Do not report “80% success” from five people; report “four of five completed”.

Stated intent is a poor predictor of behaviour. Participants will tell you they would use anything, to be polite. Observed behaviour and recalled specifics are the reliable signals; predictions about the future are not.

Analysis: the rainbow spreadsheet

The standard synthesis tool: participants as columns, observations as rows, a coloured cell where a participant hit an issue. Patterns become visually obvious, and severity falls out of the count — an issue hit by five of five participants is not a preference, it is a defect. Insist on this artifact rather than a prose summary, because prose lets a single dramatic quote outweigh four quiet failures.

Task success rate — the primary metric

The proportion of participants who achieve a pre-declared, observable outcome without assistance. Its power comes entirely from declaring the outcome before the test; declared afterwards, it is a rationalisation.

SUS — the System Usability Scale

Ten fixed statements, five-point agreement, alternating polarity, scored 0–100 (not a percentage). Its value is not the number itself but that it is comparable: thirty years of published data means a score of 68 is exactly average, and you can state where you sit against a benchmark rather than arguing about taste. Administer it unchanged — edit the wording and you lose the comparability that is the entire point.

Approve, approve-with-conditions, or send back

Approve

All twelve questions answered. Zero severity-4 findings. Severity-3 findings either fixed or waived in writing with a dated owner. WCAG AA evidence attached with measured numbers. Every state specified. Tested with real users from each distinct group.

Approve with conditions

Structure is right and the remaining gaps are bounded and named. Write the conditions as acceptance criteria with dates and owners — not as “follow-ups”, which is where conditions go to die. Re-review is scheduled at approval time, not promised.

Send back

Any severity-4 finding. Any missing state on a primary screen. No accessibility evidence. No named primary user. Labels drawn from the data model. Zero real-user contact. Send back with the specific questions unanswered — never with “it doesn’t feel right”.

One habit worth more than the other eleven

Before you look at any screen, write down — on paper, before the meeting — what you expect the dominant element to be and what the first three seconds should communicate. Then look. The gap between your written expectation and what you actually see is the review. This defends you against the single greatest risk in reviewing AI-generated work: it is persuasive, and a persuasive artifact will quietly overwrite a reviewer’s unstated expectations.

  2. Get out on time. Her shift is scheduled to end at 2:30 p.m.; she leaves at 3:10 on a good day. Anything that adds five minutes to her morning is a real cost she will actively avoid.
  3. Not be the store on the slide. District calls surface waste percentage by store. Being the worst store is a specific, named social cost she will work to avoid.
  4. Keep her crew unblocked. Two openers standing idle at 6 a.m. waiting for direction is, in her words, “the most expensive thing in the building.”
  5. Hit food cost. Genuinely cares, ranks it fifth. It is a monthly number; the other four are today.

The goal inversion that decides the design

Harborline’s objective is to reduce waste. Marisol’s objective is to never run out. These are in direct conflict, and hers is enforced by the people standing next to her. A system that simply tells her to prep less will be overridden upward, quietly and permanently. The design must therefore give her something she wants in exchange for prepping less: confidence that she will not run out. That is why the Prep Board leads with a stockout-risk statement, not with a savings figure, and why the confidence indicator is framed as “check this one” rather than as a probability.

4.5 Trust posture toward the AI

Marisol is not anti-technology; she is evidence-driven and previously burned. Her stance, paraphrased from three separate interviews: “Show me it’s been right for two weeks and I’ll stop checking. Get one Saturday wrong and I’m back on paper.”

  • Asymmetric error cost. An under-forecast that causes a stockout costs roughly ten times an over-forecast, in her personal accounting. The interface should acknowledge this asymmetry rather than pretending errors are symmetric.
  • Explanation over accuracy. She will accept a wrong number she understands faster than a right number she does not. Driver transparency buys more adoption than a two-point MAPE improvement.
  • Reputation is per-store. Trust does not transfer between stores. Rollout communication must be store-local, and a bad week at one store must not be presented as a system-wide result.

4.6 Anti-goals — things she explicitly does not want

  • To be scored, ranked, or gamified against other stores on the screen she uses to do her job.
  • To type free text at 5:41 a.m. Every observed attempt at a text field in the pilot was skipped.
  • To be asked “why did you change this?” as a required field. She will pick the first option in the list to get past it, permanently poisoning the reason data.
  • To receive push notifications during service hours.

1. Structural principles

  1. Group by the loop, not by the department. Prep and waste are separate database tables and separate HQ functions, but for a kitchen manager they are one cycle. They therefore sit side by side at the top level.
  2. The primary task is the landing screen. Today’s Prep is not a destination you navigate to; it is where the tablet already is. Depth zero for the 87% case.
  3. Maximum depth three, core tasks at depth two or less. Verified by tree test, not asserted.

  5. Two applications, one vocabulary. The tablet app and the web app are different products with different navigation, but a concept is never renamed between them.
  6. Labels come from the floor. Every label must appear in transcripts of how staff already speak, or it needs a written justification and a tree-test result.

1. Conformance statement

Formal statement — use this wording, not a percentage

As of 2026-08-27, the PrepSense tablet application (build ps-proto-0824) and web application (build ps-web-0821) do not conform to WCAG 2.1 Level AA. Forty-one of fifty applicable Level A and AA success criteria are satisfied; four are failed and five are partially satisfied. Conformance is binary. A single unsatisfied criterion means the product does not conform, regardless of how many others pass. “82% conformant” is not a claim that may be published, quoted in procurement, or stated in a VPAT.

2. Method and scope

Automatedaxe-core 4.9 across all five screens in all five states. Covered approximately 34% of the criteria in this document. Automation alone cannot evaluate focus order, label meaningfulness, reflow legibility, or whether a status message is announced usefully. Reporting a clean automated scan as conformance is the most common accessibility falsehood in enterprise software.
Manual keyboardFull core-task completion with the pointer physically disconnected. Tab, Shift+Tab, Enter, Space, arrow keys, Esc.
Screen readerNVDA 2025.1 with Firefox on the web app; VoiceOver on iPadOS 18 for the tablet app. Each core task completed end to end without sighted assistance.
Zoom and reflow400% browser zoom (equivalent to 320 CSS px width) and 200% text-only resize.
ColourAll foreground/background pairs measured with the WCAG relative-luminance formula. Values in this document are computed, not estimated.
EnvironmentalAdditionally tested on the mounted tablet at store 118 under 340–520 lux of overhead LED at 18° off-axis, with nitrile gloves. This exceeds WCAG requirements and is why several criteria are held to AAA thresholds.
Selectively adopted AAA1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced) 7:1 for body text, and 2.5.5 Target Size 44×44 exceeded to 56×56. Adopted because of glare and gloves, not for completeness.

The number that matters in the budget conversation

Total remediation effort is 8.25 engineering days. Seven of the nine findings are half-day or one-day token and markup changes. This is the routine shape of an accessibility audit result: the findings look alarming and the fixes are cheap, because the design was done with accessibility in scope. The equivalent audit on a product where accessibility was an afterthought typically produces a re-architecture, not a checklist. That difference — not the count of failures — is what you are protecting when you require WCAG evidence at design time.

6. Regression protection

  1. axe-core in CI on every pull request; any new violation fails the build.
  2. ESLint rule failing the build on outline: none or outline: 0 without an accompanying :focus-visible replacement.
  3. Contrast assertions as unit tests against the token pairs in §3, so a token change that breaks a ratio fails the test suite rather than reaching an auditor.
  4. Manual keyboard-only pass on the core task in every release candidate checklist — 4 minutes, not automatable.
  5. Screen-reader pass on the core task once per quarter and on any change to a custom component.
  6. WCAG 2.2 gap assessment scheduled 2026-10-01 (adds 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured, 2.5.7 Dragging Movements, 2.5.8 Target Size Minimum, 3.3.7 Redundant Entry — the first and third of which PrepSense already satisfies).

1. Rules of use

  1. Application code references semantic tokens only. A literal hex value or an off-ladder pixel value in a component is a defect, caught by lint, not a style preference.
  2. Primitives are referenced only by semantic tokens. They are the single place a raw value appears in the entire codebase.
  3. Component tokens require a written reason. Seven exist; each is justified in §7. An eighth needs an approved intake request.
  4. Every colour token carries its permitted use. Three of the four brand accents fail AA as text on white; the token names and the permitted use column encode that rule so nobody has to remember it.
  5. Both themes are measured independently. State colours are not symmetric across light and dark. See HK-UX-D6 §3.
  6. Renaming a semantic token is a breaking change. Deprecate, warn, migrate, then remove.

Directing AI UX work

You will mostly not be commissioning humans. You will be prompting a model, judging what comes back, and putting your name on it. The failure modes are specific and repeatable, and so are the corrections.

A brief that produces usable output

The seven-part UX brief — omit any part and the model will invent it

  1. Who, with context. Not “a manager” but “a kitchen manager with 10 months tenure, standing, gloved, 11 minutes, 5:41 a.m., under 400 lux of glare.”
  2. The one job. A single sentence with no “and also”.
  3. The environment, as measurements. Device, mount height, lighting in lux, noise in dB, connectivity reliability, glove or wet hands, interruption frequency.
  4. The constraints that are not negotiable. Minimum target size, contrast floor, offline behaviour, reading level, prohibited interactions.
  5. The design system. Paste the token names. Say that literals are prohibited.
  6. The output format, precisely. “A table with these columns” beats “be thorough” every time. Better still, paste a finished example — that is what the nine deliverables in this guide are for.
  7. What you will reject. Stating the rejection criteria up front is the highest-leverage sentence in any brief: “I will reject any screen without all five states, any colour claim without a measured ratio, and any label that came from a database column.”

Verification is not optional

A model will produce a contrast ratio, a WCAG criterion number, a tap count, and a research citation with equal fluency and unequal reliability. Three rules:

  • Recompute every number that matters. Contrast ratios, tap counts, and depth counts are cheap to check and frequently wrong. A confidently stated “4.7:1” that is actually 3.9:1 is worse than no number, because it stops the conversation.
  • Check every cited criterion against the actual text. Criterion numbers get transposed — 1.4.3 and 1.4.11 are different requirements with different thresholds, and swapping them changes the verdict.
  • Treat all research claims as unsourced until sourced. “Users prefer…” from a model means “this is common in my training data.” That is a hypothesis, sometimes a good one. It is never evidence, and it may not be recorded in a document as though it were.

Where AI is genuinely better than a human designer, and where it is not

Better: generating the complete state matrix nobody wants to write; producing twelve structural variants in the time a human produces one; drafting error copy for forty failure modes; converting a heuristic list into a scored evaluation; first-pass accessibility inspection of markup; writing the tedious annotation table.

Not better, and not close: knowing that the manager photographs the par-sheet because the PDF takes forty seconds to load. That came from standing in a kitchen at 5:38 a.m. No model has that, no prompt retrieves it, and it was the single most valuable finding in the entire discovery phase. Your job is to make sure someone goes and stands in the kitchen — and then to give the model that finding so it can do the forty things it is genuinely faster at.

Reference R2

Frequently asked questions

Twenty-two questions an architect actually asks in the first month. These are also the answers the Ask assistant draws on — type any of them into the panel and it will return the answer below with a link to the section it came from.

What is the minimum I actually need to know?

Nine things, and they are the nine sections in this guide: personas and jobs-to-be-done, user journeys, information architecture, the difference between wireframes, mockups and prototypes, Nielsen’s ten heuristics, WCAG 2.1 AA, visual and interaction hierarchy, design systems and tokens, and usability testing. If you can hold those and apply the twelve-question review lens, you can direct and approve UX work without being a designer.

If you only have an hour: learn the ten heuristics by name, learn the eight WCAG criteria that catch the most defects, and learn to ask for all five screen states. Those three habits catch the majority of real problems.

What is the difference between a wireframe, a mockup and a prototype?

They answer different questions and cost differently. A wireframe answers “is the right information in the right place, in the right priority order?” and omits colour, type and brand. A mockup answers “does the visual hierarchy make the right thing dominant, and does it conform to the design system?” and omits behaviour. A prototype answers “can a real person complete the task?” Relative cost to change is roughly 1×, 4×, 10×.

The rule that saves the most money: never let a decision be made at a fidelity higher than the decision requires. AI tools make this harder, because they generate polished output instantly — so teams now start at the wrong end of the ladder by default.

How many users do I need for a usability test?

Five per distinct user group to find roughly 85% of discoverable problems, from Nielsen and Landauer’s model 1 − (1 − L)n with a typical L = 0.31. Two caveats matter more than the number:

  • It is five per group. Kitchen managers and district managers are different groups with different tasks and contexts, so PrepSense needs five of each.
  • It applies to finding problems, not to measuring anything. A defensible task-success percentage or a comparable SUS score needs 20–30 per group. With five participants, report “four of five completed” — never “80% success”.

What contrast ratio do I need?

4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (≥18.66px bold or ≥24px regular) under WCAG 2.1 AA criterion 1.4.3. Separately, 3:1 for the visual boundary of every input, button and control, and for graphical objects needed to understand content, under 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast — the criterion most often missed.

Take headroom when the environment is hostile. PrepSense targets 7:1 for body text because the tablet sits under 340–520 lux of glare at 18° off-axis, which costs roughly 25% of effective contrast. Passing 4.5:1 in a design tool and failing in the room is still failing.

Why does the primary button here have a near-black label instead of white?

Because white on Cherry Red #FB2832 measures 3.85:1, which fails the 4.5:1 requirement for normal text. Near-black #1A1A1A on the same red measures 4.52:1 and passes at any size.

Three of the four brand accents fail AA as text on white: Cherry 3.85:1, Leaf Green 3.02:1, Amber 2.46:1. That is not a flaw in the palette — accents are mid-luminance by design, because that is what attracts attention. It is a flaw in how palettes get applied. The fix is a documented permitted-use rule per colour, encoded in semantic tokens so nobody has to remember it.

What are the five states every screen needs?

Empty (no data yet, or legitimately nothing — must teach, not apologise), Loading (specified at 200 ms, 3 s and 15 s, with a hard timeout), Partial or stale (some data old — the most-forgotten state in AI products), Error (what failed, what happened to the user’s work, what to do next), and Success (visible, durable, with a next action).

A wireframe set showing only the populated happy state specifies roughly 29% of a screen’s real behaviour. This is the most common and most expensive gap in AI-generated UX. See D4 for all five states written out with exact copy.

What is a good SUS score?

68 is exactly average across thirty years of published data. Above 80 is grade A and roughly the 90th percentile; below 51 is grade F. SUS is scored 0–100 and is not a percentage.

Its value is comparability, so administer the standard ten items with the wording unchanged — editing it destroys the only property that makes it useful. Administer it before any debrief discussion; a warm conversation first inflates the score. PrepSense moved 54 → 63 → 71 → 82 across four rounds.

How do I prioritise usability findings?

Nielsen’s 0–4 severity scale, rated on three inputs: frequency, impact, and persistence (whether users learn around it or hit it forever). 4 Catastrophe blocks release with no waiver. 3 Major blocks release unless waived in writing with an owner and a fix date. 2 Minor is scheduled within two sprints. 1 Cosmetic goes to a batched polish pass. 0 means investigated and found not to be a problem — recorded so it is not re-raised three sprints later.

A heuristic evaluation without severity ratings is a list of complaints. With them it is a schedule.

How do I tell a real persona from a stock photo with a name?

Ask: could this persona settle a design argument? “34, married, two kids, tech-savvy, enjoys hiking” decides nothing. “She will accept a recommendation she disagrees with rather than spend 90 seconds finding the override, because at 5:47 a.m. the delivery truck matters more” decides two design rules and one instrumentation requirement.

Five checks: exactly one persona marked primary; goals separable from system-imposed tasks; frustrations specific enough to name the workaround the person already invented; at least one persona who will actively resist; and a provenance line on every persona (interview count, shifts observed). No provenance means it is a proto-persona and must be labelled so nobody cites it as fact.

What is a job story and why not a user story?

Format: When [situation, with enough detail to be uncomfortable], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome that matters to me, not to the business]. The circumstance is the part a user story leaves out, and the circumstance is what generates design.

“As a kitchen manager I want to see forecasts so I can prep correctly” is unfalsifiable and produces nothing. “When it is 5:41 a.m., the produce truck is late, and the forecast wants 22% more slaw than last Thursday, I want to know in one glance what changed” produces a specific requirement: the same-weekday delta and the top driver must be on the primary screen, not one level down.

Is the three-click rule real?

Not as a law about clicks — users happily click seven times when each click feels like progress. It is a proxy for certainty per step. Use it in this form: every core task must be reachable in three taps or fewer, and at each tap the user must be able to predict what happens next.

Then verify it rather than asserting it. Count the taps from the resting state of the device, including login, context selection, and dismissing anything. See the D3 click-cost audit: ten core tasks, maximum two taps, because the most frequent task is the landing screen and costs zero.

How should an interface show model confidence?

As the action the user should take, not as a probability. PrepSense uses two states — CHECK and READY — each with an icon shape and a text label, never colour alone. Numeric confidence was tested and rejected: managers either ignored 71% or read it as “wrong”.

The governing rule: a predicted value must never be rendered with the same visual authority as a measured value. And when confidence is low, state the cause in words — “New item, only 4 days of history” — because the cause is what lets a domain expert decide whether their own knowledge beats the model’s.

Should we try to reduce the override rate?

No. Monitor it as a health band — PrepSense uses 12–18%. Sustained 0% is a red flag, not a triumph: it means the override is undiscoverable or socially discouraged, and you have bought silent compliance instead of adoption.

At Harborline, moving Adjust onto the row and renaming it from “Override” raised its use by 31% — and the forecast improved, because the overrides carried real information about the world the model could not see. A system optimised to minimise overrides would have suppressed that signal and looked more successful in the dashboard.

What is a design token, and why not just use hex codes?

A token is a named decision. Three tiers: primitives hold the raw values and are the only literals in the codebase; semantic tokens name the intent (action.primary.bg) and are what application code references; component tokens are local overrides that each require a written reason.

The naming test: read it aloud and ask could this value change without the name becoming a lie? color.red.primary becomes a lie the moment the brand changes, so teams keep the old value and drift. action.primary.bg never does. AI-generated design systems fail this constantly — they produce blue-600 ladders, which are primitives wearing a system’s clothes. A palette is not a design system.

Which WCAG criteria catch the most real defects?

Eight: 1.4.3 Contrast, 1.4.11 Non-text Contrast, 1.4.10 Reflow, 2.1.1 Keyboard, 2.4.7 Focus Visible, 2.4.3 Focus Order, 3.3.2 Labels or Instructions, and 4.1.3 Status Messages.

The single most common failure in modern web UI is 2.4.7 — a focus ring removed for aesthetics. It is always deliberate, always a one-line fix, and always found by unplugging the mouse and pressing Tab.

Does a clean automated accessibility scan mean we conform?

No, and treating it as though it does is the most common accessibility falsehood in enterprise software. Automated tooling covers roughly a third of the criteria. It cannot evaluate focus order, whether a label is meaningful, whether a data table remains legible after reflow, or whether a status message is announced usefully.

Conformance also is not a percentage. It is binary: one unsatisfied criterion means the product does not conform. “41 of 50” is progress worth reporting internally; “82% conformant” is not a claim you may publish, quote in procurement, or put in a VPAT.

Why insist on the unhappy path?

Because nobody needed design help with the good day. At Harborline the unhappy path — stale forecast, network drop mid-commit, screen contradicting the walk-in — occurs roughly two mornings in five, and only 18% of managers could tell whether their work survived it. One such failure produced an observed nine-day abandonment, a cost that appears in no error-rate metric.

Four questions to ask of any journey map: where does the data go wrong, where does the network go wrong, where does the person go wrong, and where does the organisation go wrong — and what does the interface say in each case?

How do I justify UX spend to a finance-minded executive?

Never with a UX metric alone. “SUS rose from 54 to 82” does not survive a budget review. Chain it: “SUS 54 → 82; task success 62% → 92%; adoption 82% of stores by day 30; waste down 1.4 points, which at Harborline’s food cost is $1.28M annualised.”

The UX metrics are the mechanism; the business metric is the argument. State clearly which one you are claiming causation for, and state the attribution method — a number without a stated method is a liability the first time someone challenges it.

What do I actually say in a design review?

Work the twelve questions in order. The highest-value habit, though, comes before the meeting: write down what you expect the dominant element to be and what the first three seconds should communicate — then look. The gap between your written expectation and what you see is the review.

This matters most with AI-generated work, because it is persuasive, and a persuasive artifact quietly overwrites a reviewer’s unstated expectations. Never send something back with “it doesn’t feel right”; send it back with the specific question that was not answered.

How do I brief an AI to produce usable UX?

Seven parts, and the model will invent any you omit: (1) who, with physical and cognitive context; (2) the one job, in a sentence with no “and also”; (3) the environment as measurements — lux, dB, mount height, connectivity reliability; (4) the non-negotiable constraints; (5) the design system token names, with literals prohibited; (6) the output format, precisely — ideally a finished example pasted in; (7) what you will reject.

Part 7 is the highest-leverage sentence in any brief. See R1 for the five characteristic failure modes and the correction prompt for each.

What is a dark pattern in an internal enterprise tool?

Consumer dark patterns are pre-ticked consent, confirmshaming, and cancel flows longer than sign-up flows. The internal-tool equivalent is metric laundering: making disagreement expensive so that “recommendation accepted” rises while trust falls.

It is harder to spot because nobody intends harm — someone simply had a target to move. Name it, refuse it, and replace the metric: monitor override rate as a health band rather than minimising it, and measure whether users believe the confirmation rather than whether they clicked it.

What am I accountable for, versus the designer?

The designer owns method and craft: research technique, framing options, layout, hierarchy, component choice, contrast implementation, test protocol. You own the two narrow points of the Double Diamond — Gate 1, where the problem statement is fixed, and Gate 2, where the design is approved — plus four standing judgements: that the persona set is complete, that the labels match the words the business actually uses, that every state is specified, and that WCAG 2.1 AA is a stated acceptance criterion with measured evidence rather than an aspiration.

And entirely yours, with no counterpart: how AI output is judged. AI will produce plausible UX at speed; the review lens is the only control.

Glossary

Terms you will hear in a design review, defined the way a practitioner uses them rather than the way a textbook does.

TermDefinition
AffordanceA property of a thing that signals how it can be used. A raised button affords pressing. Flat design removed many affordances and replaced them with convention, which works only for users who have the convention.
Card sortParticipants group content items into piles and name the piles. Open reveals their mental model; closed tests yours. Output is an agreement matrix showing which items are contested.
Cognitive loadThe mental effort a task demands. Split into intrinsic (the task itself), extraneous (imposed by the interface) and germane (building understanding). UX reduces extraneous load; it cannot reduce intrinsic load.
Dark patternA design that works against the user’s interest by exploiting how attention and defaults work. In internal tools, usually appears as metric laundering.
Double DiamondDiscover, Define, Develop, Deliver. Two divergence–convergence cycles. Its practical use is telling you which artifact to ask for at which moment.
Fitts’s LawTime to hit a target grows with distance and shrinks with target size. The most frequent action gets the biggest target in the easiest place to reach — which on a wall-mounted tablet is the lower-centre band, not the top-right corner.
Gestalt principlesHow the eye groups things: proximity, similarity, closure, continuity, common region. Proximity is stronger than most designers expect — bad grouping cannot be repaired with dividers.
Heuristic evaluationExpert inspection against a known list, usually Nielsen’s ten. Independent inspection then joint severity rating. Cheap, fast, and not a substitute for testing with real users.
Hick’s LawDecision time grows logarithmically with the number of equally-weighted choices. Reduce simultaneous choices, or make them unequal so the eye can discard most instantly.
Information architecture (IA)How content is grouped, labelled and structured. The invisible discipline: done well nobody notices, done badly every downstream problem gets blamed on visual design.
Job storyWhen [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]. Forces circumstance into the requirement, which is exactly what a feature request omits.
MAPEMean absolute percentage error — forecast accuracy. Relevant here because PrepSense’s MAPE never changed while task success rose 30 points. Model accuracy and system usefulness are different variables.
Moment of truthA point in a journey carrying disproportionate weight, where trust is won or lost. Usually two or three per journey, and rarely where the team assumed.
PersonaA compressed model of a user’s goals, context and constraints, precise enough to settle a design argument. If it cannot settle an argument, it is a stock photo with a name.
POURPerceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust — the four WCAG principles.
Progressive disclosureShowing what is needed now and keeping the rest one predictable step away. The test is whether the user can predict what is behind the step without opening it.
Proto-personaA persona built from assumption rather than research. Legitimate and useful, provided it is labelled, so nobody cites it as evidence later.
Rainbow spreadsheetUsability-test synthesis: participants as columns, observations as rows, a marked cell where a participant hit an issue. Patterns become visually obvious and severity falls out of the count.
ReflowWCAG 1.4.10. Content must not require two-dimensional scrolling at 320 CSS px equivalent (400% zoom). Wide data tables fail this constantly.
SEQSingle Ease Question. One 1–5 rating asked immediately after each task. Cheap, comparable across tasks and rounds.
Severity rating0–4 scale combining frequency, impact and persistence. Turns a list of complaints into a schedule.
SUSSystem Usability Scale. Ten fixed statements, five-point agreement, alternating polarity, scored 0–100 (not a percentage). 68 is average. Its only real value is comparability, so never edit the wording.
TokenA named design decision. Primitive (raw value), semantic (intent), component (justified local override). Application code references semantic tokens only.
Tree testTask completion against a text-only version of a proposed structure. Measures success, directness and first-click accuracy with no visual design in the way. A structure that fails a tree test will fail as a product.
WCAG 2.1 AAWeb Content Accessibility Guidelines, Level AA: 30 Level A plus 20 Level AA criteria. The level referenced by ADA settlements, Section 508, EN 301 549 and virtually all enterprise procurement. Conformance is binary.
