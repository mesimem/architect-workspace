Orientation

Start here

You are not being trained to build integrations. You are being trained to look at an integration an AI agent produced, in an afternoon, spanning six systems you do not own, and say with justification: this is safe to put in front of customers — or this is not, and here is the specific thing that is wrong with it.

Integration is where AI coding agents are simultaneously most useful and most dangerous. Useful, because the work is largely mechanical: read two schemas, write the mapping, wire the client, handle the codes. An agent does in twenty minutes what took a contractor three weeks. Dangerous, because an agent optimises for the request that succeeded. It will hand you a connector that works beautifully the first time it is called, on a good day, with a healthy upstream, and will silently double-charge a customer the moment a network blip causes a retry.

Almost every serious integration defect is a member of one of six families. Learn to smell these six and you will catch the majority of what an agent gets wrong:

1 · The unbounded wait

An outbound call with no timeout. One slow dependency consumes every worker thread and the whole intake queue stops. Look for a number, in milliseconds, on every single call.

2 · The duplicate side effect

A retry that fires the payment twice, sends the SMS twice, or opens two claims. Look for an idempotency key on every write, generated by the caller, stored by the receiver.

3 · The silent swallow

An empty catch, or a 200 OK returned for work that did not happen. Look for where a failure goes: a dead-letter store, a named alert, a human.

4 · The unversioned contract

A partner renames a field and your intake collapses on a Tuesday afternoon. Look for a version in the path, a schema validated at the boundary, and a contract test in CI.

5 · The invented field

The mapping document says a target field is populated, but no source produces it, so the agent defaulted it. Look for a named source for every field, or an explicit constant with a reason.

6 · The leaked secret

An API key in a query string, a bearer token in a log line, credentials in a committed .env. Look at the URL and look at the log format, every time.

The one sentence to carry

An integration is not finished when the call succeeds. It is finished when you know exactly what happens when the call fails, when it is slow, when it arrives twice, and when the thing on the other end changes shape without telling you.

The worked example

Project CLARITY

Meridian Mutual Insurance Group is a US personal-lines property and casualty carrier: 1.9 million auto and homeowner policies across 22 states, 3,400 employees, headquartered in Hartford. Project CLARITY is its first AI-assisted claims initiative — and it is, structurally, an integration programme wearing an AI badge.

The business problem

A policyholder whose car is damaged files a First Notice of Loss (FNOL). Today that takes a fourteen-minute phone call, is transcribed by an adjuster into the claims system, waits an average of 31 hours for a coverage check against the policy administration system, and waits a further two to six days before a fraud review releases the first payment. Meridian's own survey puts 68% of policyholder dissatisfaction on the first 72 hours. Competitors are settling simple glass and fender claims in under an hour.

The target: for a well-defined band of low-complexity auto claims, take FNOL from submission to an approved advance payment in under nine minutes, with no human in the loop, and route everything outside that band to an adjuster with the evidence already assembled. The AI parts — reading the damage photos, scoring the fraud risk — are the smallest part of the work. The work is the seams.

Read the awkward column again

Every entry in that last column becomes a design constraint, and each one of them is something an AI agent cannot infer from a schema. PolicyCore's batch window means the flow must be able to pause and resume. SentinelScore's 120 rpm means the flow needs a queue and a shed-load policy. DocuLens's at-least-once callbacks mean the callback handler must be idempotent. PayRail's irreversibility means the idempotency key must be derived, persisted, and auditable. Your job as architect is to supply these constraints to the agent, because nobody else will.

The eligibility band — what “straight-through” means here

A claim is eligible for automated advance payment only if all of the following hold. This is not integration detail; it is the business rule the integration exists to serve, and you should know it before reviewing anything.

  • Peril is one of COLLISION, GLASS, or ANIMAL_STRIKE; no bodily injury reported; no third party at the scene.
  • Policy in force on the loss date, premium not in arrears, coverage confirmed by PolicyCore.
  • DocuLens damage estimate below $7,500 with extraction confidence ≥ 0.82.
  • SentinelScore fraud score < 420 with no hard-stop factor code.
  • Policyholder has a verified payout method and no open claim on the same vehicle in 30 days.

Anything failing one of these is not an error. It is a routing decision — the claim goes to an adjuster with the evidence attached. Conflating “ineligible” with “failed” is one of the most common mistakes in AI-generated flows, and it produces both false alarms and missed real failures.

Foundations

Why integration exists

Integration exists because value lives in the gaps. No single system in Meridian Mutual can settle a claim: the policy truth is in a 1997 mainframe, the damage evidence is in a photo, the fraud judgement is in a model, the money moves through a gateway, and the customer only ever sees the app. The claim gets settled in the space between those systems, and someone has to own that space.

The four things an integration actually buys

What it buysWhat that means in practiceProject CLARITY instance
Elimination of human relay A person retyping data from one screen into another is an integration made of flesh: slow, expensive, and with an error rate nobody measures. Automating the relay is usually the entire business case. An adjuster currently retypes FNOL details into ClaimsHub and separately looks up coverage in a PolicyCore green-screen. 31 hours of the cycle time is queue, not work.
Single source of truth, enforced Without an integration, the same fact gets stored in three systems and diverges. With one, you designate an owner per fact and everything else reads from it. Coverage and deductible are owned by PolicyCore. CLARITY may cache them for the life of a claim, stamped with the read time, but never treats its copy as authoritative.
Capability you could not build Buying a capability through an API is how a mid-size carrier gets damage-estimation AI at all. The alternative is not building it worse; it is not having it. DocuLens and SentinelScore represent maybe 400 person-years of model work. Meridian rents them through two HTTPS seams.
Auditable decisions A well-built integration leaves a trail: what was asked, what was answered, what was decided, when, by which version of which model. This is not overhead in a regulated industry — it is the product. Every automated payment decision must be reconstructable three years later for a state insurance examiner, including the fraud model version that produced the score.

Why it is the hardest part of AI delivery

Teams consistently underestimate integration because the demo is easy and the production system is not. The gap between them is made of the following, none of which appear in a proof of concept:

The demo assumption
  • The other system is up.
  • It answers in 200 ms.
  • It answers once.
  • The fields mean what their names suggest.
  • The schema will not change.
  • One request at a time.
  • Nothing costs money.
The production reality
  • PolicyCore rejects writes for 2.5 hours nightly.
  • SentinelScore p99 is 840 ms and worse at 09:00 ET.
  • DocuLens delivers 1.4% of callbacks twice.
  • deductible is per-occurrence in one system, per-claim in another.
  • MeridianConnect shipped a new field on a Thursday.
  • Monday morning brings 6× the FNOL volume.
  • Each DocuLens call costs $0.42 and each retry costs another.
The asymmetry that should shape your review

A coding agent's training corpus is overwhelmingly composed of the happy path, because that is what people publish. It has read a million examples of fetch(url).then(r => r.json()) and comparatively few that carry a timeout, a capped retry with jitter, an idempotency key and a dead-letter route. It is not being careless. It is reproducing the distribution it learned. The correction has to come from your instructions and your review, and it will not emerge on its own.

The integration engineer's actual job

Stripped of tooling, the discipline is five activities. Recognising which one is missing is most of reviewing:

  • Define the seam. Agree, in writing, what crosses the boundary: shape, meaning, units, authority, and who may change it.
  • Translate. Move data between two models that were designed by people who never met, without losing meaning. This is the data-mapping document, and it is where quiet corruption lives.
  • Absorb failure. Decide what happens when the other side is slow, down, rate-limited, or lying. Every outbound call is a promise you cannot keep alone.
  • Preserve identity. Ensure one real-world event produces exactly one claim, one payment, and one text message, no matter how many times the message is delivered.
  • Make it observable. When a policyholder rings on Thursday about a claim from Tuesday, one identifier must reconstruct the whole journey across all eight systems.

Notice that only the second of those is about data. The other four are about failure, identity and evidence — and they are precisely the four an unsupervised agent will under-build.

1 · REST, GraphQL and event APIs

Three interaction models, not three technologies. The distinction that matters is who decides what gets fetched and who initiates.

StyleShapeStrong whenWeak whenCLARITY use
REST Server defines resources and responses. Client calls GET /claims/{id} and takes what it gets. Stable, well-understood nouns; caching matters; many unsophisticated consumers; you need HTTP semantics (status codes, conditional requests, idempotent verbs). A screen needs eleven resources at once and you are on a mobile network. Over- and under-fetching are structural. Every server-to-server seam. POST /v1/claims/intake, GET /v1/claims/{claimId}. Chosen for HTTP idempotency and status-code semantics.
GraphQL Client sends a query describing exactly the fields it wants; one endpoint, one round trip. Rich client UIs over many entities; multiple client versions with different needs; you want to change the front end without a back-end release. You need HTTP caching, simple ops, or a hard rate limit — a single malicious query can be arbitrarily expensive. Depth and complexity limits are mandatory, not optional. Only between the mobile app and the MeridianConnect BFF, where the claim-status screen needs claim, policy, vehicle and payment in one call on cellular.
Event / async Producer publishes a fact that happened. Consumers subscribe. Producer does not know or care who listens. Fan-out to several consumers; decoupling release cycles; work that need not finish inside a request; audit trails are a natural by-product. You need an answer now to continue. Eventual consistency becomes visible to users, and debugging requires real tooling. claim.intake.completed on EventMesh, consumed independently by ClaimsHub, the analytics warehouse, and Notify360. None of them blocks intake.
Review question 1

For each seam, why this style? A REST call where an event belongs creates a temporal coupling you will pay for at 3 a.m.; an event where a REST call belongs creates a user staring at a spinner that will never resolve. An agent defaults to REST for everything because REST dominates its training data.

2 · API contracts and versioning

A contract is the enforceable promise about what crosses a boundary: the fields, their types, their units, their meaning, which are optional, what the errors are, and how it will change. “Enforceable” is the operative word. A contract that lives in a Confluence page is a wish; a contract expressed as a schema, validated at the boundary, and asserted by a test in CI is a contract.

Four levels of contract strength

LevelFormConsequence when the other side changes
L0Prose documentation, or noneYou find out from a customer complaint. Typical of undirected AI work: the code is the contract.
L1Schema published (OpenAPI / JSON Schema)You find out when you read the changelog, which you will not.
L2Schema validated at runtime at the boundaryYou find out at the first bad request, with a clean 400 and a specific message, instead of a corrupt row three tables deep.
L3L2 plus contract tests in CI on both sidesYou find out before it ships, in a pull request, in the morning. This is the target for every CLARITY seam.

What counts as a breaking change

Backwards-compatible — minor version
  • Adding an optional response field.
  • Adding an optional request field with a safe default.
  • Adding a new endpoint.
  • Adding a new enum value if consumers were told to tolerate unknowns.
  • Relaxing a validation rule.
Breaking — new major version required
  • Removing or renaming any field.
  • Changing a type ("7500" to 7500) or a unit (dollars to cents).
  • Making an optional request field required.
  • Narrowing validation, or changing an error code's meaning.
  • Changing the meaning of a value while keeping its name — the most dangerous of all, because every test still passes.

CLARITY versions in the URL path (/v1/) for the public seam, because it is unambiguous in a log line and trivial for a legacy consumer. Two major versions run concurrently for a maximum of 180 days; the sunset date is announced in a Deprecation and Sunset response header from the day the successor ships, so consumers can detect their own obsolescence programmatically.

Review question 2

Show me the schema, show me where it is validated at runtime, and show me the test that fails if the other side changes. If any of the three is missing you are at L0 or L1 regardless of how much documentation exists. Then: what is the versioning scheme, and what is the deprecation window?

3 · Authentication

Four mechanisms cover essentially everything you will review. What you need is the ability to say which one is appropriate and what its specific failure mode is.

MechanismHow it worksAppropriate whenFailure mode to check
API key A long shared secret in a header. No expiry, no scope, no user identity. Server-to-server, low sensitivity, vendor does not offer better. It never expires, so a leak is permanent until someone notices. Check: is it in a header and never a query string? Is rotation possible without downtime? Is it scoped to the minimum?
OAuth 2.0

Tokens expire in minutes, limiting the blast radius of a leak. Token caching. An agent will often fetch a new token on every call — correct but wasteful and a self-inflicted rate limit — or cache it for ever and hit 401 storms. Correct: cache until 80% of lifetime, refresh proactively, and handle a single 401 by refreshing once, not by retrying forever.
OAuth 2.0
authorization code + PKCE		 A human consents in a browser; the app receives a code and exchanges it for tokens bound to that user. Any time the identity that matters is a person and their permissions. Confusing the user's token with the service's token. A policyholder's token must never be used to call PolicyCore — the orchestrator calls with its own service identity, having checked the user's authorisation for that claim.
mTLS

Where does the credential live, how does it rotate without downtime, what is its scope, and can it appear in a URL, a log line, an error message, or a stack trace? Review question 4

For every webhook: is the signature verified before the payload is trusted? Is the handler idempotent? Does it return 2xx quickly and do the work asynchronously, so the sender is not waiting on your database? What is the sender's retry policy, and what happens to an event that is never delivered at all? 5 · Idempotency, retries and backoff

This is the single highest-value concept in the guide. If you take one thing from Week 7, take this.

Idempotent means performing the operation twice has the same effect as performing it once. Reads are naturally idempotent. Writes are not, unless they are made so deliberately. And retries — which you need, because networks fail — turn every non-idempotent write into a duplicate waiting to happen.

The ambiguity at the heart of all of this

When an outbound call times out, you do not know whether it succeeded. The request may never have arrived; or it may have arrived, been processed, and the response was lost. From your side the two are identical. This is not solvable by better code — it is a property of distributed systems. The only escape is to make the operation safe to repeat. That is what an idempotency key is for.

The idempotency key, concretely

A caller-generated, stable identifier for an intent. The receiver stores it with the result of the first execution. On seeing it again, the receiver does not redo the work — it returns the original result. Three properties decide whether it actually works:

  • Derived, not random. If the key is a fresh UUID per attempt, retries carry different keys and the protection is void. In CLARITY the advance-payment key is sha256(claimId + ":" + paymentType + ":" + amountCents), so any attempt to pay the same claim the same amount presents the same key — whether it is a retry 200 ms later or a replay from the dead-letter queue three days later.
  • Persisted before the side effect, not after. Write the key with status IN_FLIGHT in the same transaction that begins the work. A key recorded after success does not protect the window that actually matters.
  • Long enough retention. PayRail honours keys for 24 hours; CLARITY retains its own record for 90 days so a dead-letter replay a week later is still caught on our side. Take the shorter of the two windows as your real guarantee — and know which one it is.

Which errors may be retried

ConditionRetry?Reasoning

Review question 5

Three parts, and all three must be answered. (a) Is every write idempotent, with a key that is derived rather than random, persisted before the side effect? (b) Is every retry capped, jittered, and limited to retryable error classes? (c) What happens after the final attempt fails — and is it something other than dropping the work? An agent will frequently produce (b) alone, which is worse than nothing, because retries without idempotency are a duplicate-generation machine.

6 · Timeouts and circuit breakers

A timeout is the maximum you are willing to wait. A circuit breaker is the decision to stop calling something that is clearly broken. The first protects a single request; the second protects the system.

Every outbound call needs an explicit timeout. The default in most HTTP clients is either none or something absurd like two minutes; either way, one slow dependency will exhaust your connection pool and take down functionality that has nothing to do with it. Set the timeout from the dependency's observed p99 plus headroom — not from a guess, and not from a round number that feels generous.

The timeout budget rule

Timeouts compose. If a caller's own deadline is 3 s and it makes three sequential calls with 2 s timeouts each, it can spend 6 s producing a result nobody is waiting for any more. Each layer's timeout must be strictly shorter than its caller's remaining budget. CLARITY propagates a deadline in the X-Deadline-Ms header and refuses to start a call it cannot finish in time — a fast, honest failure instead of wasted work.

Review question 6

Point me at the timeout on every outbound call — a number, in the code or config, not a default. Then: what is each one derived from? Then: for each dependency whose loss would stop the flow, what is the breaker threshold, the cooldown, and the fallback the business takes while the circuit is open? “It returns an error to the caller” is an answer only if someone has agreed that the customer sees an error.

8 · Rate limits

A cap the other side imposes on how often you may call. Exceeding it earns 429 Too Many Requests, and if you respond by retrying immediately you have built a self-sustaining outage.

ConceptWhat it means and what to check
Fixed window120 calls per clock minute. Simple, and permits a double-rate burst across the boundary — 120 at 10:00:59 and 120 more at 10:01:01.
Sliding window120 calls in any trailing 60 seconds. Smoother, and the common vendor implementation. SentinelScore uses this.
Token bucketA bucket of 120 tokens refilling at 2/second; a call costs a token. Permits a controlled burst then settles to the sustained rate. The right model for your client side.
Concurrency limitNot a rate at all — a cap on simultaneous in-flight requests. PolicyCore allows two concurrent sessions per service account. Your connection pool must be capped at two or you will get intermittent, confusing rejections.
Retry-AfterThe header telling you exactly how long to wait. Honour it. An agent frequently ignores it in favour of its own backoff, which is both rude and slower.
Client-side limitingThe mature move: never exceed the limit in the first place. Queue at the boundary, shape the flow, and shed the lowest-value work first when saturated.

CLARITY's SentinelScore seam is the binding constraint. The limit is 120 rpm; peak FNOL arrival on a Monday morning after a winter storm reaches roughly 310 per minute. The design therefore cannot be “call the API when a claim arrives”. Instead: claims enter a bounded queue, a limiter releases 110 per minute (an 8% safety margin below the cap), and if the queue depth exceeds 400 the flow sheds deliberately — claims route to MANUAL_FRAUD_REVIEW rather than waiting. The customer still gets a claim; it just is not automatic. That trade-off is a business decision and it is recorded in the architecture document, not buried in a config file.

Review question 8

What is every dependency's published limit, what is our expected peak, and what happens in the gap? If the answer is “we retry on 429”, the design is incomplete: retrying is recovery, not a capacity plan. Ask for the limiter, the queue bound, and the shed policy — and ask who agreed to what gets shed.

9 · Error handling and dead-letter queues

The purpose of error handling is not to prevent errors. It is to ensure that no unit of work disappears silently. Every failed item ends in exactly one of four places, and you should be able to name which, for any failure, before approving anything.

Error classification

Every caught exception gets a stable error_class string before it is logged. Generic Error is not a classification; it makes triage impossible and it hides the difference between a transient blip and a contract break. CLARITY's vocabulary is fixed and small:

TimeoutError RateLimitError AuthError ValidationError ContractViolation UpstreamUnavailable DuplicateSuppressed BusinessRuleDeclined DeadlineExceeded CircuitOpen

BusinessRuleDeclined and DuplicateSuppressed earn their place by not being failures. An ineligible claim and a suppressed duplicate are the system working correctly. Alerting on them trains the on-call engineer to ignore alerts, which is how real incidents get missed.

Review question 9

Name the destination for every failure class. Then interrogate the dead-letter queue itself: who owns it, what alerts on it, has the replay path been tested, and is the replay idempotent? A DLQ nobody watches is a slower form of losing the work — and a DLQ whose replay double-pays is worse than no DLQ.

10 · Synchronous versus asynchronous

The question is simply: does the caller wait? It sounds like an implementation detail. It is the single most consequential decision in the whole design, because it determines what a user sees when something goes wrong.

Choose synchronous whenChoose asynchronous when
The caller genuinely cannot continue without the answer — an authorisation decision, a coverage check that determines the next screen. The work takes longer than a user will wait, or longer than a sane HTTP timeout.
The work completes in well under a second and the dependency is highly available. Several consumers need the same fact, and you do not want to know who they are.
Eventual consistency would be visible and confusing to the user. You need the work to survive a deployment, a crash, or a dependency being down for an hour.
There is no reasonable way to notify the user later. Load is spiky and you want a queue to absorb the peak rather than shedding it.
Review question 10

For each seam: does the caller wait, and if so what does the user see at the p99 of that wait? Multiply the availabilities of every synchronous dependency in the chain — is the product acceptable? If the answer to “what does the user see when this dependency is down” is “a spinner, then an error”, the seam probably wants to be asynchronous.

11 · Orchestration versus choreography

Once you have several async steps, there are two ways to arrange them, and mixing them carelessly is how a system becomes impossible to reason about.

Orchestration — a conductor

One component owns the flow and calls each step in order. The sequence lives in one readable place; the state of any claim is a single row you can query.

  • Easy to answer “where is claim 44812 and why?”
  • Easy to add a compensating action when step 4 fails.
  • The orchestrator becomes a dependency of everything and a bottleneck if careless.

CLARITY uses this for the intake flow. In a regulated industry, being able to reconstruct a decision path is worth more than the coupling costs.

Choreography — a dance

Each component reacts to events and emits its own. No one owns the flow; it is an emergent property of the subscriptions.

  • Maximum decoupling; add a consumer without touching anything.
  • No bottleneck, no single point of failure.
  • The flow exists in no single place. Debugging requires tracing, and “why did nothing happen?” is a genuinely hard question.

CLARITY uses this for everything downstream of the decision: analytics, the warehouse, and customer comms all subscribe to claim.intake.completed and none of them can block a claim.

The useful heuristic

Orchestrate the flow that has a deadline and an owner. Choreograph the consequences that do not. If an agent gives you an event-driven design for a flow with a nine-minute SLA and a regulator, ask where the state machine is — because there has to be one somewhere, and if it is implicit it is unqueryable.

13 · Secrets at the seam

Integrations are where credentials live, which makes them the most common source of leaked secrets. Four rules, all absolute, all cheap to verify.

  • Never in a URL. Query strings appear in access logs, proxy logs, browser history, referrer headers and error-tracking screenshots. Credentials go in headers, always. This is a thirty-second check that catches a real, recurring AI-generated mistake.
  • Never in a log line. Not at debug level, not “temporarily”. Log a prefix if you must correlate (ik_9f3a…), never the value. Assume every log is retained for seven years and readable by more people than you expect.
  • Never in the repository. Not in a committed .env, not in a test fixture, not in a comment showing “an example”. A secret committed once is compromised permanently, because git history is for ever — rotate first, then clean the history.
  • Rotatable without downtime. If rotating a key requires a maintenance window, it will not be rotated, and an unrotated key is one long-lived leak away from an incident. Support two valid credentials during the overlap.

And the one that is specific to this domain: PII is not a secret but it is not free either. CLARITY logs no policyholder name, no VIN, no address, no photograph, and no full payment token. It logs identifiers that are meaningless outside the system. A claim ID is enough to investigate; a name is a liability.

Review question 13

Show me a real log line from this seam at debug level, and show me the outbound URL. Then: where is the credential stored, how is it rotated, and what is its scope? If the answer to any of those is a shrug, the security architect's sign-off is not obtainable and you should not be offering yours.

Judgement

Good versus bad integration

Both of the following pass their tests. Both were produced by a capable coding agent. One of them will cost Meridian Mutual a regulatory finding and a seven-figure remediation. The difference is visible in under ten minutes if you know where to look.

The same seam, built twice

The seam is the advance payment: the orchestrator instructs PayRail to disburse money to a policyholder. Read the bad version first and see how reasonable it looks.

What an undirected agent produces
async function payAdvance(claim) {
  const res = await fetch(
    `${PAYRAIL}/payments?api_key=${KEY}`, {
    method: 'POST',
    body: JSON.stringify({
      amount: claim.estimate,
      account: claim.payoutAccount
    })
  });
  if (!res.ok) {
    console.log('payment failed', await res.text());
    return null;
  }
  return res.json();
}
  • Secret in the URL. That key is now in PayRail's access logs, our egress proxy logs, and any error screenshot.
  • No timeout. A hung PayRail hangs this worker until the process is restarted.
  • No idempotency key. Any retry — by a caller, a queue, or a human clicking twice — pays twice.
  • No retry at all, so a single TCP reset silently loses a customer's payment.
  • Units unstated. claim.estimate is dollars; PayRail expects cents. This pays 1/100th of the amount, and it will look like a rounding bug for a month.
  • Returns null on failure, so the caller cannot distinguish “declined” from “network blip” from “we may have paid”.
  • Logs the raw error body, which contains the masked account number and the request echo.
  • No correlation ID, so this payment cannot be tied to its claim in any log search.
What a directed agent produces
async function payAdvance(claim, ctx) {
  const key = idemKey(claim.id, 'ADVANCE',
                      claim.estimateCents);
  await idemStore.reserve(key, claim.id);   // before
  return withPolicy(SEAMS.payrail_authorize, ctx, {
    request: {
      url: `${PAYRAIL}/v3/payments`,
      method: 'POST',
      headers: {
        'Authorization': `Bearer ${await token()}`,
        'Idempotency-Key': key,
        'X-Correlation-ID': ctx.correlationId,
        'X-Deadline-Ms': ctx.remainingMs()
      },
      body: {
        amount_cents: claim.estimateCents,   // unit explicit
        currency: 'USD',
        destination_token: claim.payoutToken
      }
    },
    timeoutMs: 5000,
    attempts: 4,
    backoff: { baseMs: 250, jitter: 'full', capMs: 8000 },
    retryOn: [ECONNRESET, ETIMEDOUT, 502, 503, 504, 429],
    onDuplicate: (prior) => prior.result,    // 409 = done
    onExhausted: (err) => dlq.put('payrail', claim, err, ctx)
  });
}
  • Derived idempotency key, reserved before the call, so a duplicate is impossible even across a process restart.
  • Credential in a header, short-lived, and never logged.
  • Explicit timeout and a capped, jittered retry over a named set of retryable conditions.
  • Units in the field name. amount_cents cannot be confused with dollars by the next reader or the next agent.
  • A duplicate is success, not an error — PayRail's 409 returns the original result.
  • Exhaustion has a destination: the dead-letter queue, with full context, replayable.
  • Correlation ID and deadline propagated, so the call is traceable and will not outlive its purpose.
  • Policy is declarative and shared, so every seam is consistent and reviewable in one file.
What produced the difference

Not a smarter model. The right-hand version is what you get when the prompt says: every outbound call carries an explicit timeout and a capped jittered retry; every write carries a derived idempotency key persisted before the side effect; failures route to a dead-letter store; no secret appears in a URL or a log; monetary fields name their unit. The quality came from the instruction. See Directing the AI for the full text.

The five marks of a good one

  • Boring in production. The best integration is one nobody has thought about for six months. Drama is a design smell, not a fact of life.
  • Failure is a routine, designed path — not an exception. Somebody has already decided what happens, written it down, and tested it.
  • Any single item can be traced, and any failed item can be replayed by someone who was not involved in building it, using a documented procedure.
  • The contract is explicit and enforced, so an upstream change breaks a build in the morning rather than a night of data.
  • The degraded mode is a business decision. When SentinelScore is down, claims go to manual review — and Claims Operations agreed to that, in advance, in writing.

1Purpose and scope

This document defines the integration architecture for Project CLARITY: the automated intake, assessment and advance payment of low-complexity personal auto claims. It is the authoritative description of what crosses each boundary, in which direction, under what contract, with what guarantees.

In scope: the Clarity Intake Orchestrator and the eleven seams it owns; the delivery mode, contract, timeout, retry and idempotency posture of each; the degraded behaviour of the flow when any dependency is unavailable; the correlation and observability model.

Out of scope: the internal design of any dependency system; the fraud model's construction (owned by Decision Science, see MMI-DS-MOD-014); the adjuster workbench user experience; the eligibility thresholds themselves, which are a Claims Operations policy recorded in MMI-CLM-POL-009 and consumed by this architecture as configuration.

Governing principle

The orchestrator is the only component that may hold the flow. Every dependency is assumed to be slow, unavailable, or duplicated at some point in any given week, and the architecture is required to remain correct — not merely alive — under each of those conditions. Correctness here means: no claim lost, no claim stuck, no duplicate payment, and every outcome explainable.

Note on S11 and the outbox pattern

Publishing an event and committing a state change are two side effects that must agree. If the state commits and the publish fails, downstream systems never learn; if the publish succeeds and the commit rolls back, they learn of something that did not happen. CLARITY writes the event into an outbox table inside the same database transaction as the state change, and a relay process publishes from that table and marks rows sent. This is the only pattern in this architecture that guarantees the two cannot diverge, and it is required on every seam that publishes a fact about a state change.

For each seam, the choice of mode is a decision with a reason, not a default. This table is the answer to “why is this one synchronous?”.

The shed decision — ADR-006, and it is a business decision

When the SentinelScore queue exceeds 400 claims, CLARITY stops queuing and routes new claims directly to MANUAL_FRAUD_REVIEW. The alternative — queue everything and preserve automation — would mean a claim filed at the Monday morning peak might wait 25 minutes for a fraud score, breaching the 9-minute commitment silently. Claims Operations (P. Adeyemi) accepted this trade-off on 2026-09-02 and staffs the manual queue accordingly. Shedding load without a named owner who agreed to receive the work is not a capacity plan; it is a transfer of your problem to someone who does not know they have it.

2Versioning and deprecation

The major version is in the path (/v1/). Minor and patch versions are additive and are not expressed in the path; consumers learn the served version from the X-API-Version response header.

Change classExamplesHandling
PATCHBug fix; documentation correction; performance changeDeployed without notice. Behaviour under the contract is unchanged.
MINORNew endpoint; new optional request field; new response field; new enum value; new error code in an existing categoryDeployed with a changelog entry and a notice to registered consumers 7 days ahead. No consumer action required.
MAJORField removed or renamed; type or unit changed; optional field becomes required; error code meaning changed; enum value removed; any change to the meaning of an existing valueNew path (/v2/). Both versions served concurrently for 180 days minimum. Deprecation announced from the day v2 ships.

Deprecation signalling

From the moment a successor version ships, every response from the deprecated version carries machine-readable notice, so a consumer can detect its own obsolescence without reading an email:

HTTP/1.1 200 OK
X-API-Version: 1.4.0
Deprecation: version="v1"; date="2027-01-15"
Sunset: Sat, 15 Jul 2027 00:00:00 GMT
Link: <https://developer.meridianmutual.com/clarity/migrate-v2>; rel="deprecation"
Warning: 299 - "Intake API v1 is deprecated and will be withdrawn on 2027-07-15. See Link header."
The rule that matters most in this section

Changing the meaning of a value while keeping its name and type is a major change, and it is the one that passes every automated check. If fraudScore is rescaled from 0–1000 to 0–100, no schema notices, no test fails, and every claim is approved. Semantic changes are versioned here with the same severity as structural ones — and for SentinelScore specifically, the model version is carried in the response and validated against a known-good list before any automated approval.

5.4  GET /v1/payments/by-key/{idempotencyKey}

Added in 1.4.0 and the single most important operational endpoint in the specification. Reads a payment back by the idempotency key that created it, which is how a PAYMENT_UNCERTAIN claim is resolved without ever re-attempting a payment blind.

This is what an idempotency key buys you beyond deduplication

The key is not only a guard against doing the work twice. It is a handle on the work — a name you can use to ask “did this happen?” after a timeout. A design with idempotency keys but no read-by-key endpoint can prevent duplicates but cannot resolve uncertainty, and so every timeout on a money seam becomes a manual investigation. Ask for this endpoint by name whenever you review a payment integration.

5.5  POST /v1/claims/{claimId}/reprocess

Supervisor-only. Replays a dead-lettered claim from its last good state. Requires claims:reprocess, requires an Idempotency-Key, and is fully audited. Replay is idempotent by construction: it re-executes from persisted state using the original derived keys, so a completed payment is not repeated.

6Inbound webhooks

Two vendors call us. Both contracts follow the same five rules, which are the rules any inbound webhook in this estate must follow.

#RuleImplementation
1Verify the signature before parsingHMAC-SHA256 over the raw request body, compared in constant time. The body is not deserialised, logged, or acted on until the signature verifies. Parsing untrusted input before authenticating it is the vulnerability.
2Reject stale and replayed deliveriesSigned timestamp must be within 300 seconds of server time. The (source, eventId) pair has a unique constraint, so a replay is a no-op rather than a reprocess.
3Acknowledge fast, process laterReturn 204 within 500 ms after enqueuing. Never hold the vendor's connection while calling PolicyCore — that couples their retry policy to our slowest dependency.
4Be idempotentHandlers are safe to run repeatedly. DocuLens delivers ~1.4% of callbacks twice; this is normal at-least-once behaviour, not a vendor defect.
5Have a pull-based backstopA 15-minute sweep finds any job past its deadline with no callback and reads the result directly. A webhook that never arrives is otherwise invisible.

Is there a number — an actual millisecond value — against every seam? Is every retry capped and jittered and restricted to a named error class? Is the idempotency key derived, and is it persisted before the side effect? Does the failure-mode catalogue include a column for failures that are explicitly not handled? That last column is the mark of an honest design: a document claiming to handle every failure mode is a document nobody has stress-tested.

1Principles

#PrincipleConsequence
P1No unbounded wait.Every outbound call carries an explicit timeout derived from the dependency's observed p99. No client default is ever relied upon.
P2No unbounded retry.Every retry sequence has a maximum attempt count, a jittered backoff and a cap. Infinite retry is prohibited without exception.
P3No duplicate side effect.Every write carries a derived idempotency key, reserved before the call. Duplicate suppression is enforced by a database constraint, never by a read-then-write check.
P4No silent loss.Every unit of work reaches a terminal state or sits visibly in the dead-letter queue. An empty catch block is a production defect.
P5No undeclared degradation.Every fallback is agreed with the named business owner who receives the resulting work, in advance, in writing.
P6No unexplainable outcome.One correlation ID reconstructs the full journey, including every failed attempt and the reason for each.
P7Uncertainty is a state, not an error.Where an outcome is genuinely unknown — a timeout on a write — the state machine says so, and a reconciliation resolves it. Guessing is prohibited.

2Timeout design

Timeouts are derived, not chosen. The rule: timeout = observed p99 × 1.5, rounded to a sensible value, then sanity-checked against the caller's remaining deadline. A timeout longer than the caller's patience is a wasted resource; a timeout shorter than the dependency's normal p99 is a self-inflicted failure.

2.1  Deadline propagation

Timeouts compose badly. A caller with a 3-second deadline making three sequential 2-second calls can spend 6 seconds producing an answer nobody is waiting for. CLARITY propagates a deadline:

// Every seam call checks the remaining budget before it starts.
const remaining = ctx.deadlineMs - elapsed();
if (remaining < seam.timeoutMs) {
  if (remaining < seam.minimumViableMs) {
    throw new DeadlineExceeded(seam.id);   // fail fast and honestly; do no wasted work
  }
  effectiveTimeout = remaining - GUARD_MS; // shrink to fit rather than overrun the caller
}
The connection-pool interaction, which is where timeouts actually bite

PolicyCore permits two concurrent sessions per service account. With a 4-second timeout and two slots, the theoretical maximum throughput of S2 is 30 calls per minute — comfortably above the observed mean of 52 claims per minute only because most claims do not need a fresh read. This constraint is the reason S2 has an admission deadline of 6 seconds in front of the pool: a request that cannot even acquire a connection in 6 seconds fails immediately rather than queueing invisibly. A design that sets a timeout without considering the pool will appear to work in test and collapse at peak.

3.1  Classification — what may be retried

Read the payrail_authorize block carefully

ETIMEDOUT is absent from its retryOn list, and this is the single most important line in the document. On every other seam a timeout is retryable. On the money seam a timeout means the outcome is unknown, and the correct response is not another attempt but a state change and a reconciliation read. An agent will add ETIMEDOUT to this list by default, because it is retryable everywhere else, and the result is a duplicate-payment machine that passes every test.

3.4  Retry budgets

A per-seam budget caps retries at 10% of total calls over a rolling 60 seconds. Beyond the budget, retries are suppressed and calls fail straight to their fallback. This exists because retries are the mechanism by which a struggling dependency is pushed over: at a 50% failure rate with 3 attempts, an unbudgeted client generates 2× its normal load precisely when the dependency can least absorb it. Budget consumption is a dashboard metric; current use is 31% of the allowance.

Blocking review finding RF-002, recorded here permanently

Version 1.0 of this design used crypto.randomUUID() per attempt as the idempotency key. This is what the coding agent produced, and it reads as correct: the header is present, the vendor honours it, every test passes. It is completely ineffective. A fresh key per attempt means every retry is, to PayRail, a new intent — so the guard never engages and the retry policy becomes a duplicate-payment generator. The finding was raised by D. Okonkwo on 2026-07-12 and blocked sign-off until v1.1. “Is the key derived or random?” is the single highest-yield question in an integration review.

5.3  Why the guard is a constraint, not a check

Racy — what an agent writes
const seen = await db.findEvent(eventId);
if (seen) return reply(204);          // ← gap
await db.insertEvent(eventId);
await processExtraction(payload);

Two simultaneous deliveries both read “not seen” before either writes. Both proceed. The claim is scored twice and, downstream, may be paid twice. This passes every sequential test and fails a few times a month in production, which is the worst possible failure profile: rare enough to look like a fluke, frequent enough to be expensive.

Correct — the database serialises the decision
try {
  await db.insertEvent(eventId);      // UNIQUE (source, event_id)
} catch (e) {
  if (isUniqueViolation(e)) {
    metrics.inc('duplicates_suppressed');
    return reply(204);                // success, deliberately
  }
  throw e;                            // never swallow the unexpected
}
await processExtraction(payload);

The uniqueness decision is made by the only component able to serialise it. There is no window. Note also that an unexpected error is re-thrown rather than treated as a duplicate — a catch that swallows everything would turn a database outage into silent data loss.

StepActionDetail
1Enter PAYMENT_UNCERTAINOn timeout or attempt exhaustion on S8. No further authorize attempt is made under any circumstances.

Incident INC-2026-0910, and why the age alert exists

On 10 September a DocuLens client secret expired. 38 claims dead-lettered with AuthError in fourteen minutes; the depth alert fired at 25, the secret was rotated, and all 38 replayed successfully within the hour. The depth metric returned to normal and the incident was closed. One item did not replay — a claim whose document set had meanwhile been deleted by a policyholder account action — and it sat in the queue for six hours and forty minutes, invisible, because depth had recovered. The age-based alert (CLAR-812) was added as a direct result. Depth tells you about floods; age tells you about neglect, and the second is the one that quietly harms an individual customer.

1Purpose and conventions

This document is the authoritative mapping for Project CLARITY. Where code and this document disagree, this document is correct and the code is a defect — the mapping assertions in the test plan enforce that direction of authority.

ConventionRule
Every field has a sourceA specific system and field name, or an explicit constant with a documented reason, or a derivation with a written formula. “Derived” alone is not an acceptable entry and will be rejected at review.
Units are namedMonetary target fields end in Cents. Distance ends in Miles. Duration ends in Ms or Seconds. No bare amount, ever.
Null vs absentTreated identically on input. On output, an absent value is omitted rather than sent as an empty string or a zero — zero is a real amount and must never be a sentinel.
PrecisionNo floating-point arithmetic on money at any point. Vendor decimals are converted to integer minor units at the boundary, once, with explicit rounding.
Unknown enum valuesNever silently defaulted. Every crosswalk in §4 has an explicit unknown row, and the behaviour is to route to manual review.
DirectionIN = into the canonical model; OUT = from canonical to a downstream system.

2Canonical model

All four inbound sources map into one internal canonical claim, and all outbound systems map out of it. This matters: with a canonical model there are 4 + 2 mappings; without one there are up to 12 point-to-point translations, and a vendor change ripples into every downstream system rather than stopping at one mapper.

5Unit and precision rules

5.1  DEF-0441 — the dollars-to-cents defect, recorded permanently

Found in UAT on 2026-08-26. Estimated exposure had it reached production: $1.6m in underpayments in the first month.

Version 1.4 of this document recorded M-31 as a pass-through: result.estimate.amount → estimateCents. The vendor's field is named amount with no unit, inside an object named estimate, and the example in their documentation used 4128 — a whole number that reads perfectly well as either $4,128.00 or 4,128 cents. The agent that generated the mapper chose cents, consistent with our own convention, and every unit test passed because the fixture used the same assumption as the code.

A single UAT claim with an estimate of 4128.50 exposed it: the value arrived as a decimal, which cents cannot be. The true behaviour is dollars as a JSON decimal. The correct mapping parses the decimal as a string, multiplies by 100, rounds half-up, and asserts an integer result. Every claim before the fix would have paid roughly 1% of the amount owed.

Controls added as a result: (1) every monetary target field name ends in its unit; (2) the mapping test suite asserts on a non-round vendor value, 4128.50, specifically because a whole number cannot distinguish the two interpretations; (3) a range assertion rejects any advance below $50 or above $7,500 before it reaches PayRail; (4) the review checklist requires the unit of every monetary field to be confirmed against a live vendor response, not against documentation.

5.2  Numeric rules

RuleDetail
PolicyCore padded stringsFixed-width, zero-padded, whole dollars: "0000050000" is $50,000. Strip leading zeros, parse as integer dollars, multiply by 100. An empty string is not zero — it means the field was not returned, and that routes to manual review.
Sentinel valuesPolicyCore uses "9999999999" for "unlimited" and "-000000001" for "not applicable". Both map to null. A mapper that treats them as numbers produces a $99.9m policy limit.
RoundingHalf-up, applied once, at the boundary. Never mid-calculation. All internal arithmetic is on integers.
Floating pointProhibited for money anywhere in the pipeline. Vendor decimals are parsed from their string form to avoid an intermediate float.
Outbound to ClaimsHubDollars as a string with exactly 2 decimals, e.g. "4128.00". Generated by integer division and modulo, never by float division.

5.3  The deductible granularity mismatch

PolicyCore holds the deductible per occurrence; ClaimsHub holds it per claim. One hailstorm damaging two insured vehicles is one occurrence and two claims. A naive mapping attaches the full deductible to both claims and underpays the policyholder by one deductible; the inverse error overpays.

Rule, from Claims Operations (P. Adeyemi, 2026-08-01): where deductiblePerOccurrence is true and an occurrence spawns multiple claims, the deductible attaches to the earliest-created claim only; subsequent claims from the same occurrence carry deductibleApplied = 0.00 and a reference to the claim bearing it. Occurrence identity is (policyNumber, lossDate, peril). In release 1 this condition is detected and routed to manual review rather than automated, because the occurrence-linking logic is not yet trusted for automated payment. This is a deliberate scope decision, not an omission.

Directing the AI

The difference between the two implementations in good versus bad was not the model. It was the instruction. An agent will build the integration you described; the entire skill is describing the one you would be willing to approve.

The standing preamble

Put this in front of any integration task. It is the compressed form of this whole guide, and it moves an agent from producing a demo to producing something reviewable. Keep it in a file and reuse it.

You are building an integration between systems I do not control. Apply these rules
without being asked again, and state explicitly where you could not apply one.

CALLS
- Every outbound call has an explicit timeout in milliseconds, with a comment saying
  what it was derived from. Never rely on a client default.
- Every retry is capped, jittered (full jitter), and limited to a named list of
  retryable error classes. Unbounded retry is prohibited.
- Never retry 400, 422, or a repeated 401 after one token refresh.
- Honour Retry-After on 429 and 503 in preference to your own backoff.
- Propagate a deadline and refuse to start work that cannot finish inside it.

WRITES
- Every write carries an idempotency key that is DERIVED from the intent, not random,
  and is persisted BEFORE the side effect, not after.
- Duplicate suppression is enforced by a database unique constraint, never by a
  read-then-write check.
- On a timeout on a money or irreversible write: do not retry. Enter an explicit
  uncertain state and resolve it with a read-by-key reconciliation.

FAILURE
- No empty catch. No success response for work that did not happen.
- Every failure has exactly one destination: retry, a named fallback, or a
  dead-letter store with the original payload, correlation id, idempotency key,
  error class and attempt history.
- Tag every caught exception with a stable error_class before logging it.
- Circuit-break anything whose loss would stop the flow, and tell me what the
  business does while the circuit is open.

CONTRACT
- Validate inbound and outbound payloads against a schema at the boundary.
- Reject unknown fields on writes rather than discarding them silently.
- Version the contract and state what would constitute a breaking change.

SECURITY
- Credentials in headers only. Never in a URL, a log line, or an error message.
- Verify inbound webhook signatures over the raw body, before parsing, in constant
  time, with a timestamp replay window.
- Log identifiers, never PII. Assume every log line is retained for seven years.

MONEY AND UNITS
- Integer minor units only. The unit is in the field name (amountCents).
- No floating-point arithmetic on money anywhere.
- Convert vendor decimals to minor units once, at the boundary, with explicit rounding.

OUTPUT
- Produce the timeout/retry/idempotency posture as a table before writing code.
- List every assumption you made about the other system's behaviour.
- List every failure mode you did NOT handle.

Five follow-up prompts that find what was left out

Use these after the agent says it is done. In practice each one finds something.

1 · The inventory

“List every outbound call you made, in a table, with its timeout, maximum attempts, backoff curve, retryable error classes, idempotency key derivation, and what happens when the last attempt fails. Mark any cell you had to leave blank.”

Blank cells are the findings. An agent will fill what it can and the gaps are where it improvised.

2 · The duplicate

“For each write, trace exactly what happens if the same message is delivered twice, concurrently, within 50 ms. Show me the code path that prevents a second side effect and say whether it is a database constraint or an application check.”

“An application check” is the answer you are hunting for, and it is a race.

3 · The blackout

“Assume each dependency in turn is completely unavailable for thirty minutes. For each one: what does the customer see, where does the work go, who is alerted, and what manual action is needed on recovery?”

This surfaces every seam with no fallback, and every alert routed nowhere.

4 · The leak

“Show me a real log line at debug level for each seam, and the exact outbound URL. Then list every field in those that would be a problem in a seven-year retained log read by an auditor.”

Catches keys in query strings and PII in debug output in about a minute.

5 · The confession

“List every assumption you made about the other systems' behaviour that you could not verify, and every failure mode you chose not to handle. Rank them by what they would cost if you are wrong.”

The most valuable prompt in the set. Agents answer it honestly and it is faster than finding the same gaps by reading.

6 · The unsourced field

“For every field you populate in an outbound payload, name the source system and source field, or the constant and its reason. Flag any field you defaulted, guessed, or derived without a written formula.”

This is how DEF-0441-class defects are caught before UAT rather than during it.

Practice

FAQ

Twenty-two questions in the form they are usually asked. Every answer here is also reachable through the Ask assistant at the bottom right, and through the search box.

What exactly is a “seam”?

A seam is one boundary between two systems, in one direction, with one contract. Not a system and not a connection — a contract crossing. The orchestrator calling PolicyCore for coverage is one seam (S2); the orchestrator polling PolicyCore for endorsements is a different one (S3), because it has a different mode, timeout, failure behaviour and purpose, even though it is the same two systems and the same protocol.

Counting seams rather than systems is what makes an integration reviewable. Project CLARITY has eight systems and eleven seams, and the eleven is the number that matters: each one needs its own timeout, retry policy, idempotency posture and fallback.

Should this be REST or GraphQL?

For server-to-server seams, REST, almost always. You get HTTP status-code semantics, idempotent verbs, conditional requests and caching, and every client library already understands it. For a rich client UI that needs many entities in one round trip over a mobile network, GraphQL earns its complexity.

CLARITY uses REST for all eleven server-to-server seams and GraphQL only between the mobile app and the portal's own backend-for-frontend, where the claim-status screen needs claim, policy, vehicle and payment together. If an agent proposes GraphQL for a machine-to-machine seam, ask what over-fetching problem it is solving — and check for depth and complexity limits, without which a single query can be arbitrarily expensive.

Webhook or polling?

Webhook when you need freshness and can operate a public authenticated endpoint. Polling when you cannot, when the other side offers nothing, or when you want to be immune to your own downtime.

The correct answer for anything important is usually both: a webhook for latency, and a pull-based reconciliation sweep as a backstop. A webhook that never arrives is, from your side, indistinguishable from an event that never happened — unless something independently asks. CLARITY runs a 15-minute sweep behind every push seam, and it is the mechanism that catches the callback lost during a rolling deploy.

See concept 4 for the full comparison, including the ordering and duplicate properties people forget.

What is an idempotency key, and why must it be derived rather than random?

A caller-generated, stable identifier for an intent. The receiver stores it with the outcome of the first execution; on seeing it again, it returns the original result instead of redoing the work.

It must be derived — computed from the intent, such as sha256(claimId + ':ADVANCE:' + amountCents) — because a retry must present the same key. If the key is a fresh UUID per attempt, every retry looks like a new intent and the guard never engages. This is the most common serious defect in AI-generated integration code: the header is present, the vendor honours it, every test passes, and it protects nothing. It was blocking review finding RF-002 on CLARITY, recorded in error design §5.1.

Two further requirements: the key is persisted before the side effect, and its retention outlives the longest possible replay delay.

Is it safe to retry after a timeout?

Only if the operation is idempotent. A timeout means the outcome is unknown: the request may never have arrived, or it may have arrived, been executed, and the response lost. From your side those are identical, and no amount of better code distinguishes them.

For a read, retry freely. For a write with a derived idempotency key, retry safely. For a write without one, retrying is how duplicate payments are created. And on an irreversible seam, the right answer is often neither: CLARITY deliberately excludes ETIMEDOUT from PayRail's retryable list and instead enters PAYMENT_UNCERTAIN and resolves it with a read-by-key reconciliation. See sequence flow 5.

How do I know whether a timeout value is right?

It should be derived, not chosen. The working rule is the dependency's observed p99 multiplied by about 1.5, then sanity-checked against the caller's remaining deadline. If someone cannot tell you what a timeout was derived from, it was guessed.

Two further checks. First, plot the timeout on the same chart as the latency: a p95 sitting at 70% of the timeout means the p99 is already failing (see chart 4). Second, timeouts compose — each layer's timeout must be shorter than its caller's remaining budget, or you spend six seconds producing an answer nobody is waiting for.

When do I actually need a circuit breaker?

When a dependency's failure would otherwise consume your resources and delay its own recovery. A breaker protects two parties: you, from spending connections on calls that will fail, and the dependency, from a retry storm.

The key question in review is not the threshold but the fallback: what does the business do while the circuit is open? A breaker with no fallback merely converts a slow failure into a fast one without deciding anything. CLARITY's SentinelScore breaker routes claims to MANUAL_FRAUD_REVIEW — a path Claims Operations agreed to staff, in writing, in advance. Note also that CLARITY deliberately uses different thresholds per seam; a uniform threshold everywhere is a sign nobody considered the individual fallbacks.

What makes a dead-letter queue useful rather than decorative?

Four things. Content: the original payload verbatim, the correlation ID, the idempotency key, the error class, the attempt history, and the state it failed in. An owner: a named person on a rota, not a team alias. Alerts on two dimensions: depth and oldest-item age, separately. A tested replay path, and the replay must be idempotent, because replaying is by definition a duplicate delivery.

The age alert is the one people miss. CLARITY's incident on 10 September cleared a 38-item spike within the hour, depth returned to normal, and one item sat unresolved for six hours and forty minutes because nothing alerted on age. Depth catches floods; age catches the single stuck item that quietly harms one customer.

Should this seam be synchronous or asynchronous?

Ask what the user sees at the p99 of the wait, then multiply the availabilities of every synchronous dependency in the chain. Five dependencies at 99% each give you 95.1% — roughly 36 hours of downtime a month. If that product is unacceptable, or if the work can outlast a sane HTTP timeout, the seam wants to be asynchronous.

Asynchronous is not free: it costs you a durable state machine, a status endpoint, a notification path, and the discipline of designing for a user who is no longer watching. CLARITY paid all four, because the DocuLens step alone can take 90 seconds and no synchronous design survives that.

What counts as a breaking change?

Removing or renaming a field; changing a type or a unit; making an optional request field required; narrowing validation; removing an enum value; changing an error code's meaning. All of those require a new major version.

And the dangerous one: changing the meaning of a value while keeping its name and type. If a fraud score is rescaled from 0–1000 to 0–100, no schema notices, no test fails, and every claim is approved. This is why CLARITY validates SentinelScore's model version against an allow-list and blocks automated approval on an unrecognised one — and why the residual risk of an unchanged version string with rescaled output is the largest untested risk in the programme (gap G-03).

I have twenty minutes to review an AI-generated integration. What do I check?

In this order. One: search the code for timeout values — if there are none, stop and send it back. Two: find every write and check for an idempotency key, then ask what it is derived from. Three: grep for empty catch blocks and for any place a caught exception is followed by a success response. Four: read one outbound URL and one debug log line for credentials. Five: ask where a failed item goes and who is alerted. Six: count the failure-path tests.

Those six checks take about twenty minutes and catch the large majority of what an undirected agent gets wrong. The full fourteen-point version is in the review lens.

What should happen on a 429?

Wait exactly as long as Retry-After says, then retry — not your own backoff, which is both ruder and usually slower. Ignoring that header is a common agent behaviour.

But retrying on 429 is recovery, not a capacity plan. If you are seeing 429s routinely, the design is missing a client-side limiter. CLARITY self-limits to 110 rpm against SentinelScore's 120 rpm cap, queues up to 400 claims, and sheds beyond that to manual review rather than queueing indefinitely — a trade-off Claims Operations agreed to, because a 25-minute silent wait would breach the nine-minute commitment without telling anyone.

The data map looks clerical. Why is it the highest-risk document?

Because unit errors, enum gaps and time-zone losses all live there, and none of them look like bugs. A factor-of-100 monetary error passes every type check, every schema validation and every test built on the same assumption as the code.

CLARITY's DEF-0441 is the canonical example: DocuLens returns dollars as a JSON decimal in a field named amount, and the vendor's documentation example used 4128 — a whole number that reads equally well as $4,128.00 or 4,128 cents. The generated mapper chose cents. Every claim would have paid about 1% of what was owed. It was caught in UAT only because one test claim had a non-round value. The control that followed: monetary tests use non-round values, because a whole number cannot distinguish the two interpretations.

How many failure tests is enough?

There is no universal number, but the ratio is diagnostic. CLARITY's integration tier has one happy-path case against thirty failure, boundary, security, idempotency and capacity cases. For an integration that is the right shape, because the risk is concentrated in the failure paths.

More useful than counting is checking what each case asserts. A test that asserts a 200 will pass against code that pays twice. Every write case must assert the exact count of each side effect: “exactly one payment row”. And duplicate-delivery tests must run concurrently — a sequential duplicate test cannot detect the check-then-act race it exists to catch. That was blocking finding RF-004 on this project.

Orchestration or choreography?

Orchestrate the flow that has a deadline and an owner; choreograph the consequences that do not.

CLARITY orchestrates the intake flow, because in a regulated industry being able to answer “where is claim 44812 and why?” from a single queryable state row is worth more than the decoupling. It choreographs everything downstream of the decision — analytics, the warehouse, customer comms all subscribe to claim.intake.completed and none of them can block a claim. If an agent hands you a fully event-driven design for a flow with a nine-minute SLA, ask where the state machine is: there has to be one, and if it is implicit it is unqueryable.

What is a correlation ID and why is it the first thing to check?

A UUID generated at the entry point of a request or job and propagated through every log line, every outbound header, every queue message, every event and every state row. It is the difference between a three-minute investigation and a three-day one.

The test is concrete: hand someone a claim ID and ask them to show you every log line, in order, across every system. If it takes more than five minutes, the observability claim is not true regardless of how many dashboards exist. Correlation IDs are also the most commonly missing element in AI-generated integrations, because nothing fails without them.

What usually goes wrong with OAuth in generated code?

Token caching, in both directions. Either the agent fetches a fresh token on every call — correct but wasteful, and a self-inflicted rate limit on the auth server — or it caches one for ever and produces 401 storms when it expires.

Correct behaviour: cache until about 80% of the token's lifetime, refresh proactively, and handle a single 401 with one refresh and one retry. Not a retry loop: a second 401 is a configuration fault, and retrying just logs the same error repeatedly. The other frequent error is conflating identities — a service acting on a user's behalf needs both its own token to authenticate and the user's to authorise, and must not substitute one for the other.

How should an inbound webhook be verified?

HMAC over the raw request body, compared in constant time, before the body is parsed, logged or acted on — parsing untrusted input before authenticating it is the vulnerability. Plus a signed timestamp with a replay window (CLARITY uses 300 seconds), and a unique constraint on the event ID so a replay is a no-op.

Then two behavioural rules: acknowledge fast and process asynchronously, so the sender is not holding a connection while you call your slowest dependency; and return 2xx for a suppressed duplicate rather than an error, because telling the vendor its delivery was a duplicate only invites another retry.

A vendor says they guarantee exactly-once delivery. Can I rely on it?

No. Over a network, exactly-once delivery is not achievable — what is achievable is at-least-once delivery plus deduplication, which produces exactly-once processing. When a vendor says exactly-once they mean the second thing, and the deduplication is either theirs or yours.

Design as though duplicates will arrive, because they will. DocuLens's contract says at-least-once and its observed duplicate rate is 1.4% — about 530 a month. That is not an edge case; it is a daily occurrence, and the handler must be a non-event.

Which four numbers should I be able to recite for any integration I approved?

Integration success rate per seam; end-to-end cycle time p95; duplicate side-effect rate (zero, for money); and dead-letter depth and oldest age. Those four tell you whether it works, whether it is fast enough, whether it is correct, and whether anyone is watching.

Then two leading indicators for when something feels wrong: retry rate against its budget, which moves before the success rate does, and latency plotted against the timeout rather than in isolation. And one proof-of-life metric: duplicate suppressions. If a system processing 40,000 claims a month reports that its idempotency guard has never fired, the guard is not wired in.

When I sign off an integration, what am I actually certifying?

Not that it works. You are certifying that you know how it fails: that every failure mode has been identified, that each one has a designed destination, that the degraded modes were agreed by the people who receive the resulting work, and that someone specific will be told when it happens.

That is a claim you can defend to a regulator, an auditor or a board. “The tests passed” is not — and on an integration, a green suite that contains no failure cases is actively misleading, because it reports a confidence nobody earned.

Glossary

The vocabulary you need in the room. Terms you will hear from an engineering team, defined the way an architect needs them rather than the way a specification does.

At-least-once delivery
A guarantee that a message will arrive, possibly more than once. The realistic default for webhooks and queues. Implies your handler must be idempotent.
Backoff
The increasing delay between retry attempts. Exponential with full jitter is the standard; jitter is what prevents synchronised retry storms.
Backpressure
Signalling upstream to slow down when you cannot keep up, rather than accepting work you will drop. A bounded queue with a shed policy is backpressure; an unbounded queue is denial.
BFF (backend for frontend)
A thin service serving one client type, shaping data for that client's screens. Where GraphQL usually belongs.
Circuit breaker
A guard that stops calling a failing dependency after a threshold, fails fast for a cooldown, then probes with a single trial call. Needs a fallback to be a design rather than a gesture.
Contract test
A test asserting that producer and consumer still agree on a schema, run in both sides' CI, so an upstream change breaks a build rather than a night of data.
Correlation ID
A single identifier propagated through every log line, call, message and state row for one transaction. The basis of all cross-system debugging.
Dead-letter queue (DLQ)
A durable store for work that could not be processed, holding enough context to diagnose and replay it. An inbox, not a graveyard — it needs an owner, alerts on depth and age, and a tested replay.
Deadline propagation
Passing the caller's remaining time budget down the call chain so no layer starts work it cannot finish. Prevents wasted effort on abandoned requests.
Exactly-once processing
The achievable goal: at-least-once delivery combined with deduplication. Distinct from exactly-once delivery, which over a network is not achievable.
FNOL
First Notice of Loss. The insurance term for the initial report of a claim — the event Project CLARITY exists to process.
Idempotency key
A caller-generated, stable identifier for an intent, allowing a receiver to recognise a repeat and return the original result. Must be derived rather than random, and persisted before the side effect.
Idempotent
Performing the operation twice has the same effect as performing it once. Natural for reads; deliberate for writes.
Jitter
Randomness added to a retry delay. Full jitter draws uniformly from [0, base × 2ⁿ]. Without it, all clients retry in unison and prevent recovery.
mTLS
Mutual TLS: both sides present certificates, so the transport itself proves identity. Common on high-value seams. Certificate expiry is an outage with a date on it.
OAuth 2.0 client credentials
The machine-to-machine flow: a service exchanges an ID and secret for a short-lived token. No user is involved.
Orchestration
One component owns the sequence and calls each step. Queryable state, single point of coordination. Contrast with choreography, where each component reacts to events and no one owns the flow.
Outbox pattern
Writing an event into a table inside the same transaction as the state change, then publishing from that table with a relay. The only pattern where state and event cannot diverge.
Polling
Repeatedly asking whether something has changed. Wasteful but firewall-friendly and immune to your own downtime. The correct choice when the other side offers no push.
Rate limit
A cap on call frequency imposed by the other side. Exceeding it earns 429. Fixed window, sliding window and token bucket are the common algorithms; concurrency limits are a separate constraint.
Reconciliation
Independently comparing two systems' views and resolving the difference. The answer to an unknown outcome after a timeout, and the backstop behind every push-based seam.
Retry budget
A cap on retries as a share of total traffic, typically 10%. Stops a retry policy from doubling load on a dependency at the moment it can least absorb it.
Retry-After
A response header stating how long to wait before retrying. Honour it in preference to your own backoff.
Seam
One boundary between two systems, in one direction, with one contract. The unit of integration review.
Shedding
Deliberately declining lower-value work under saturation so higher-value work still completes on time. A business decision requiring a named owner for whatever gets shed.
System of record
The authoritative source for a given fact. When two systems disagree, the system of record wins by definition and the other must reconcile.
Thundering herd
Many clients retrying simultaneously after a failure, preventing recovery. What jitter and breakers exist to prevent.
Timeout
The maximum time you are willing to wait for a call. Derived from the dependency's observed p99, never a client default and never a round number that feels generous.
Token bucket
A rate-limiting algorithm permitting a controlled burst then settling to a sustained rate. The right model for your own client-side limiter.
Webhook
An inbound HTTP call the other system makes to tell you something happened. Requires a public authenticated endpoint, signature verification, duplicate tolerance, and a pull-based backstop.
