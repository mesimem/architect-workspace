Field Guide

Project Management for the AI Solution Architect

You will not run projects day-to-day. You will direct, evaluate, and approve AI-generated project plans — charters, schedules, risk logs, budgets — before they reach a sponsor or a delivery team. This guide teaches the roughly 20% of project management discipline that gives you the judgment to do that well, then shows every document in that 20% built out in full for one concrete initiative so you can see what "good" looks like.

How this guide is built
Section one below teaches the concepts. Section two applies every concept to a single running example — an AI claims-intake copilot at a mid-size insurer — with real documents you can open, search, print to PDF, or export to CSV. Worked Example — Insurance

ClaimsIQ: AI-Powered FNOL Intake & Triage Copilot

Every document in this guide is written for one real-feeling initiative. Read this brief once — it is the context every downstream artifact assumes.

The organization

Meridian Mutual Insurance Group is a regional property & casualty insurer writing auto and homeowners policies across 12 states, processing roughly 4,200 First Notice of Loss (FNOL) claims per month through a phone intake center and a web self-service portal.

The problem

FNOL intake takes an average of 22 minutes per claim, severity triage is inconsistent between intake staff, and 14% of claims are mis-routed to the wrong adjuster queue, driving SLA breaches and roughly $2.1M/year in adjuster overtime.

The initiative

ClaimsIQ is an AI copilot embedded in the intake workflow that transcribes and structures FNOL calls and web submissions, extracts key entities (policy number, loss date, location, injury flag), scores severity/complexity, and recommends a routing queue and advisory reserve range — with a licensed adjuster confirming or overriding every recommendation. No claim decision is ever fully automated; this is a regulatory constraint in every state Meridian writes in, not a design preference.

Discipline

Why Project Management Exists

An AI model can generate a schedule, a risk log, and a budget in seconds. It cannot tell you whether that schedule is honest, whether that risk log is complete, or whether that budget has room to fail safely. That judgment is what project management discipline gives you. It exists because unmanaged work drifts: scope creeps quietly, risks go unnamed until they become issues, and "on track" status reports paper over problems until they surface at the worst possible moment — usually right before go-live. The discipline below is not process for its own sake; each piece maps directly to a failure mode it prevents.

The Scope / Schedule / Cost Triangle

Every project is constrained by three variables that trade against each other: scope (what gets built), schedule (when it ships), and cost (what it consumes). Quality sits in the middle — it degrades when any leg is stretched without adjusting the others. You cannot add scope for free; you either extend the schedule, add cost, or cut quality. When you review an AI-generated plan, the first question is always: which leg of the triangle is fixed, and does the plan actually respect that?

Review lens
For ClaimsIQ, cost is the fixed constraint ($1.2M authorized, escalation required above $1.5M). Schedule has limited flex (20 weeks, tied to a competitive response deadline). Scope is the release valve — the Phase 2 items (Spanish-language intake, commercial lines) were deliberately cut from Phase 1 scope specifically so cost and schedule could hold. A plan that quietly protects schedule by expanding budget without saying so is a plan that has broken the stated constraint.

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

A WBS decomposes the total scope of a project into progressively smaller, ownable pieces of work, until each piece is small enough to estimate, assign, and track to completion. The defining test of a good WBS: its lowest-level work packages sum to exactly 100% of scope — nothing implied, nothing double-counted. It answers "what has to get built," not "in what order" (that is the schedule's job) or "who does it" (that is the RACI's job).

Review lens
When an AI tool hands you a WBS, add up the percentages. If they sum to 94% or 108%, the plan is hiding either forgotten scope or double-counted work — do not accept it until it reconciles to 100%. See the full ClaimsIQ WBS in Document 02.

Critical Path

The critical path is the longest sequence of dependent tasks from start to finish — it determines the earliest possible completion date. Tasks on the critical path have zero float (zero slack): if any one of them slips by a day, the entire project slips by a day. Tasks off the critical path have float and can absorb some delay without moving the finish line. Naming the critical path is not optional decoration on a Gantt chart — it tells you exactly where to spend your risk-management attention and where you can safely tolerate a slip.

Review lens
A plan that has not identified its critical path has not actually been scheduled — it is a list of dates with no logic behind them. See the critical path marked in cherry red in Document 03.

Milestones & Gates

A milestone marks a significant point in time — it has no duration and consumes no effort by itself. A gate is a milestone with teeth: an explicit go/no-go decision with named exit criteria that must be met before work is authorized to continue. Gates are where governance actually happens. A gate without written exit criteria is not a gate — it is a date on a calendar that people call a gate.

Review lens
ClaimsIQ has three real gates: Compliance & Data Governance (Week 3), Model Accuracy (Week 15, ≥90% extraction accuracy required), and Go/No-Go for production (Week 18). Each has explicit exit criteria in Document 03. If a plan calls something a "gate" with no measurable exit criteria attached, push back before approving it.

RAID: Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies

RAID is the discipline of naming what could go wrong, what you're taking on faith, what has already gone wrong, and what you're waiting on from someone else — before those things ambush the schedule.

Risk

A future event that may happen and would hurt the project if it did. Scored by likelihood × impact.

Assumption

Something believed true without proof, used to allow planning to proceed. Must be validated or it silently becomes a risk.

Issue

A risk that already happened. It is real, present-tense, and needs a resolution owner now, not a mitigation plan.

Dependency

Work this project needs from an outside party (a vendor, another team) that it does not control directly.

Review lens
Every RAID row must have exactly one named owner and a date — a risk owned by "the team" is a risk owned by no one. See the full ClaimsIQ RAID log in Document 04, including how a real vendor-integration risk (Guidewire sandbox delay) is being actively managed, not just logged and forgotten.

RACI Matrix

A RACI matrix maps every key activity against every role and assigns one of four relationships: Responsible (does the work), Accountable (owns the outcome and answers for it — there can be only one), Consulted (gives input beforehand, two-way conversation), and Informed (told afterward, one-way notice). The single hardest rule, and the one most plans get wrong: exactly one Accountable person per activity, never zero, never two. Two accountable people is the same as zero — when something breaks, everyone can point at someone else.

Review lens
Scan a RACI column-by-column, not just row-by-row: if one person is "R" on eleven of thirteen activities, that is a bus-factor risk, not an efficient plan. The full ClaimsIQ RACI grid is in Document 05.

Agile vs. Waterfall vs. Hybrid

Waterfall sequences work into distinct phases (requirements → design → build → test → deploy), each completed before the next begins. It suits work with fixed, well-understood requirements and hard compliance gates, where re-work is expensive and sequencing is genuinely linear. Agile delivers work in short, fixed-length iterations (sprints), re-planning and re-prioritizing every cycle based on what was actually learned. It suits work with evolving requirements, where the fastest way to find the right answer is to build something small, show it, and adjust. Hybrid uses waterfall's phase gates for the parts of a project that are genuinely sequential and non-negotiable (legal review, data governance approval, production cutover) and agile sprints for the parts that benefit from iteration (model development, UI design) — inside the same overall plan.

Review lens
ClaimsIQ is deliberately hybrid: Phase 0 (Discovery & Compliance) and Phase 6 (Go-Live) run waterfall because regulatory sign-off cannot be iterated on, while AI model development runs seven two-week sprints because "what accuracy can this model actually hit" is something you discover by building, not by planning upfront. A plan that forces model development into a single waterfall phase with one estimate is a plan that has not accounted for how ML actually gets built.

Velocity & Burndown

Velocity is the amount of work (usually measured in story points) a team reliably completes per sprint, calculated from actual history — never from aspiration. It is used to forecast how much future work a team can commit to, typically averaged over the last 3 sprints to smooth noise. A burndown chart plots remaining work against time within a single sprint: an "ideal" line drawn straight from the starting total to zero, against the "actual" line the team is really tracking. A burndown that sits stubbornly above the ideal line for the first half of a sprint and then drops sharply at the end is a classic warning sign — it usually means work wasn't actually being completed incrementally, it was being marked done in a batch near the deadline.

Review lens
See ClaimsIQ's actual Sprint 3 burndown in Document 07 — a realistic pattern with a mid-sprint lag that the team recovered from, not a suspiciously perfect straight line.

Status Reporting & RAG

Status reports exist to surface problems while they are still cheap to fix. The standard shorthand is RAG — Red, Amber, Green — applied at both the overall project level and per workstream. Green means on track against baseline, no escalation needed. Amber means at risk — a specific, named issue is affecting schedule, cost, or scope and is being actively mitigated, but has not yet caused a miss. Red means off track — a baseline has already been missed or will be, and escalation is required. A good status report always answers four things: what changed since last time, what's the honest RAG (not the hopeful one), what decisions does the sponsor need to make right now, and what's the plan to recover anything that's Amber or Red.

Review lens
The most common failure mode an architect must catch: a status report that stays Green while the underlying metrics (SPI, CPI, defect counts) are clearly trending the wrong way. Green should be earned by the numbers, not asserted by the narrative. See Document 07 for a report that is honestly Amber and says why.

Good vs. Bad Plans

Good plan

  • WBS sums to exactly 100% of scope
  • Estimates carry a stated buffer or range, never a bare single point
  • Critical path is named and explicitly protected
  • Every RAID and RACI row has exactly one owner
  • Gates have written, measurable exit criteria
  • The fixed constraint of the triangle is stated out loud
  • Status is Green only when the metrics support it

Bad plan

  • Percentages that don't reconcile, or scope with no owner
  • Single-point estimates with no acknowledged uncertainty
  • No stated critical path — every task looks equally urgent
  • Risks owned by "the team" or left blank
  • Gates that are just dates with no pass/fail test
  • All three triangle legs implicitly treated as fixed
  • Status stays Green until the week it turns Red

The Architect's Review Lens

When an AI system hands you a project plan, you are not there to re-derive it from scratch — you are there to stress-test it fast. Run every plan through this checklist before you approve it:

  1. Does the WBS sum to 100%? If not, scope is missing or double-counted.
  2. Is the fixed constraint of the triangle stated, and does the rest of the plan respect it?
  3. Is the critical path named, and is it the thing getting the most risk-management attention?
  4. Does every RAID and RACI row have exactly one owner and a date?
  5. Do gates have written exit criteria, not just a date?
  6. Do estimates carry a range or buffer, not a suspiciously round single number?
  7. Does the status RAG match what the underlying metrics (SPI/CPI/defects) actually say?
  8. Is there a named recovery plan for anything Amber or Red — not just an acknowledgment?

This checklist is the 20% that generalizes across every AI-generated plan you will ever review, regardless of industry. Everything from here forward is that checklist applied to one concrete project, end to end.
