Solution Architect Field Guide · Week 2 Deep Dive

Directing, Evaluating & Approving AI-Generated Technical Designs

You will not spend your career hand-drawing every box on every diagram. You will spend it deciding whether the box an AI agent just drew is the right box — and whether the trade-off it quietly made on your behalf is one you're willing to sign your name to. This guide is the ~20% of solution architecture that makes that judgment possible.

What "the 20%" means here

Solution architecture as a full discipline is enormous — distributed systems theory, every cloud vendor's service catalog, a decade of pattern literature. You do not need all of it to do your job. Your job is to direct an AI system toward a sound design, evaluate what it proposes, and approve or reject it with a defensible rationale. That requires exactly the following, covered in depth in this guide:

  1. Quality attributes — because "does it work" is not the same question as "does it work at 3am during flu season under load."
  2. Architecture styles — so you can tell when an AI has over-engineered a simple problem or under-engineered a hard one.
  3. The C4 model — a shared visual vocabulary so a diagram means the same thing to you, your engineers, and your auditors.
  4. Architecture Decision Records — the paper trail that turns "the AI suggested it" into "here is why we chose it, and here is what we gave up."
  5. Trade-off analysis — because every architecture decision is a trade, never a free win, and your job is to make the cost visible.
  6. Patterns, fitness functions, and build-vs-buy — the recurring shapes and guardrails that let you evaluate a design quickly instead of from first principles every time.

The organization

Meridian Health Network operates 3 emergency departments and 9 urgent-care/primary-care clinics across a mid-sized metro region. It runs a commercial EHR platform, referred to in this guide as MeridianChart, integrated across all facilities via HL7v2 ADT feeds and a FHIR R4 API. Identity is federated through Okta; patient SMS/voice reminders already run on a licensed Twilio account. Infrastructure is mid-migration from an on-premise data center to Azure, under an existing Microsoft Enterprise Agreement.

The initiative

MedFlow AI is a clinical decision-support platform (never decision-making — this distinction matters enormously and drives ADR-002 later in this guide) that:

  • Lets patients complete structured symptom intake via a conversational web/mobile app before or upon arrival.
  • Produces a triage acuity recommendation — never an autonomous decision — for the triage nurse to review, confirm, or override.
  • Routes acknowledged cases to the correct care-team queue automatically, reducing manual handoff delay.
  • Writes the outcome back into MeridianChart via the existing HL7v2/FHIR integration layer.
Why this example is a good teaching case

It has everything a solution architect actually has to reason about in one place: hard real-time-ish latency targets, a regulated data class (PHI), a legacy system you don't get to rewrite, a build-vs-buy call on AI infrastructure, and a governance boundary (avoiding FDA SaMD reclassification) that directly shapes the technical design. It is not a toy CRUD app — and it is not so large that it can't be held in your head after 20 minutes of reading.

The Architect's 20%

Why Solution Architecture Exists

Every system that works in a demo and fails in production failed for the same underlying reason: someone solved the functional problem and never named the non-functional one. Solution architecture is the discipline of naming it first.

A business requirement says what the system must do. It almost never says how fast, how available, how secure, how much it may cost to run, or what happens when a dependency fails. Left unstated, those properties don't disappear — they get decided by accident, usually at the worst possible moment, by whichever engineer (or AI agent) happened to write that piece of code. Solution architecture exists to make those decisions on purpose, in advance, in writing, so they can be reviewed before they become expensive to undo.

Without a solution architect

  • Non-functional requirements are discovered in production incidents, not design reviews.
  • Every service picks its own patterns; the system has no coherent shape.
  • "Why did we build it this way?" has no answer six months later.
  • AI-generated code ships fast and wrong at the same rate it ships fast and right.

With a solution architect

  • Quality attributes are specified with measurable targets before a line of code is written.
  • Every significant decision has a written record of what was rejected, and why.
  • Trade-offs are visible and owned, not accidental and invisible.
  • AI proposals are evaluated against explicit criteria — not vibes.

Your specific job, stated plainly

In an AI-accelerated delivery model, the AI agent (Claude Code or otherwise) can draft a Solution Architecture Document, generate C4 diagrams, propose ADRs, and write an NFR spec faster than any human. What it cannot do is decide, on your organization's behalf, which trade-off is acceptable and which one crosses a line — that requires context the AI doesn't have: your compliance posture, your team's operational maturity, your actual budget, your risk appetite. You direct it toward the right questions, you evaluate its answers against the criteria in this guide, and you are the name on the approval.

A caution about AI-generated architecture

An AI system will produce a plausible-sounding architecture for almost any prompt, including ones with contradictory or impossible requirements. Plausibility is not correctness. The review lens later in this guide is specifically designed to catch the failure mode where a design "sounds right" but hasn't actually been checked against a measurable target.

The Architect's 20%

Quality Attributes (Non-Functional Requirements)

A quality attribute is any property of the system that is not "what it does" — it's "how well it does it, under what conditions, and how you'd know if it stopped." An NFR without a number attached is an opinion, not a requirement.

Performance

Latency and throughput under specified load. Always stated as a percentile (p95, p99), never an average.

Availability

Uptime as a percentage, with a stated measurement window and what counts as "down."

Scalability

The load ceiling the system must sustain, and how gracefully it degrades past that ceiling.

Security

Confidentiality, integrity, and access-control guarantees — and what regulatory regime enforces them.

Reliability / DR

RPO (how much data you can afford to lose) and RTO (how long you can afford to be down).

Maintainability

How cheaply the system can be changed safely — deploy frequency, change failure rate, blast radius.

Observability

Whether a failure can be diagnosed from logs/traces alone, without guessing.

Usability / Accessibility

Standards-based, testable targets (e.g. WCAG 2.1 AA), not "should be intuitive."

The rule that makes an NFR real

If you can't test it, it isn't a requirement yet

"The system should be fast" is not testable. "p95 intake response time under 2.0 seconds at 150 concurrent sessions, verified by load test" is. Every quality attribute in the MedFlow AI NFR Specification (Deliverable 04) follows this shape: requirement → measurable target → verification method. When you evaluate an AI-generated NFR spec, the first thing to check is whether every row survives that test.

How quality attributes drive architecture, not the other way around

The order of operations matters. Quality attributes are decided first, from the business requirements and regulatory context. The architecture style, the patterns, the technology choices — all of that is downstream, chosen because it satisfies the quality attributes, not chosen first and then rationalized. In MedFlow AI, the 99.95% availability target on the triage-critical path and the FDA SaMD compliance boundary are what rule out a simpler monolithic design (see Architecture Styles and ADR-001) — not architectural taste.

The Architect's 20%

Architecture Styles: Monolith vs. Services vs. Event-Driven

There is no "best" architecture style — only the style that best fits the load shape, team topology, and failure-isolation needs of the specific problem. Your job when reviewing an AI proposal is to check whether the chosen style is justified by those three things, or just chosen by default.

StyleBest fit when…Watch out for
Monolith (single deployable)Small team, low-to-moderate scale, requirements still shifting fast, deployment simplicity matters more than independent scaling.Becomes a bottleneck once teams or load scale past what one deploy pipeline and one failure domain can handle.
Service-Oriented / MicroservicesIndependent scaling needs per component, independent team ownership, different reliability tiers for different capabilities (exactly MedFlow AI's shape — see NFR-AVAIL-01 vs NFR-AVAIL-02).Operational overhead multiplies. A microservices architecture for a two-person team is over-engineering, not rigor.
Event-DrivenProducers and consumers should be decoupled in time, multiple downstream systems need the same fact (an intake completing, a score being produced), and you need replayability/auditability of what happened and when.Eventual consistency has to be an explicit, reviewed decision — not a surprise discovered in production. Debugging a chain of async events is harder than a synchronous call stack.
Serverless / FaaSSpiky, unpredictable load; you want to pay only for actual execution; startup latency is not on the critical path.Cold-start latency, execution time limits, and vendor lock-in on triggers/bindings.

Why MedFlow AI is event-driven microservices, not a monolith

Three quality attributes forced the decision, documented formally in ADR-001:

  • Differentiated availability tiers. The Nurse Triage Console and Triage Scoring Service need 99.95% uptime; the Patient Intake BFF only needs 99.9% (it has a manual paper-intake fallback). A monolith can't offer two different SLAs for two halves of itself.
  • Multiple independent consumers of the same fact. When intake completes, the Triage Scoring Service, the Audit & Compliance Service, and (eventually) analytics all need to know — independently, without one blocking another. That's the textbook case for an event bus.
  • Independent deployability under a hybrid team. The HL7 interface engine is operated by an integration engineering team with different release cadence and change-control requirements than the AI/ML services. Coupling their deploys would violate NFR-MAINT-01.
The over-engineering trap

Event-driven microservices is the right answer for MedFlow AI because three specific NFRs demand it. It would be the wrong answer for, say, a single internal reporting dashboard used by six people. If an AI-generated design reaches for microservices and you can't point to the specific quality attribute that requires it, that's a finding — send it back.

The C4 Model

C4 (Context, Container, Component, Code) gives every stakeholder — from a CMIO to a junior engineer — the right level of detail for their question, without forcing everyone to read the same overloaded diagram.

LevelAnswersAudienceUsed in this guide
1. ContextWhat is this system, and who/what does it talk to?Executives, business stakeholders, new team membersDeliverable 02
2. ContainerWhat are the deployable/runnable pieces, and how do they communicate?Architects, tech leads, ops/SREDeliverable 02
3. ComponentInside one container, what are the major structural building blocks?Engineers working inside that containerDeliverable 02
4. CodeClass/module-level detail.The engineer writing the code — generally left to the IDE, not a hand-maintained diagram.Out of scope for this guide — generated on demand, not maintained as a static artifact.
Reviewer's rule of thumb

If a Context diagram has more than ~10 boxes, it's actually a Container diagram wearing a Context diagram's label — push detail down a level. If a Component diagram spans multiple containers, it's not a Component diagram at all — it's a Container diagram missing a boundary. This single check catches a large share of AI-generated diagram sprawl.

The Architect's 20%

Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

An ADR is a short document that captures one significant, hard-to-reverse decision: what was decided, what else was considered, why the alternatives were rejected, and what the decision costs. It is the single most important artifact for making an AI-assisted architecture process auditable.

Standard ADR shape

FieldPurpose
StatusProposed / Accepted / Superseded — never silently changed without a new ADR.
ContextThe forces in tension: the requirement, the constraint, the quality attribute that's driving a decision.
DecisionThe one sentence that states what was chosen, stated as a commitment, not a suggestion.
Rejected AlternativesEvery option seriously considered, and the specific reason each one lost. This is the part AI-generated ADRs most often skip — insist on it.
ConsequencesBoth the benefit gained and the cost incurred. An ADR with no negative consequences listed has not been thought through.
Why ADRs matter more, not less, in an AI-assisted workflow

When a human architect makes a decision slowly, the reasoning tends to live in their head and in Slack threads. When an AI agent proposes a decision in seconds, there is no slow deliberation to fall back on — the ADR is the deliberation, written down where it can be reviewed before it's acted on. An AI-generated design with no ADRs is not a fast design; it's an unreviewable one.

See Deliverable 03 for six full ADRs written for MedFlow AI, including the one that keeps this system out of FDA medical-device territory.

The Architect's 20%

Trade-off Analysis

Every technology choice is a bundle of pros and cons scored against criteria that matter to this system, not against an abstract "best practice" ranking. A trade-off matrix makes that scoring explicit and repeatable.

How to build one

  1. Name the decision precisely. Not "pick a database" — "pick the event backbone for cross-service routing events."
  2. List real candidates — options someone would actually consider, not straw men included to make the preferred option look good.
  3. Choose criteria and weight them before scoring any option, so the weighting reflects what matters to the system, not what favors your preferred answer in hindsight.
  4. Score each option against each criterion on a consistent scale (this guide uses 1–5), with a one-line justification per score.
  5. State the losers' cost, not just the winner's benefit. A trade-off matrix that only explains why the winner is good hasn't done the trade-off part.
What to distrust in an AI-generated trade-off matrix

Watch for criteria weights that were clearly reverse-engineered to justify a pre-chosen winner (e.g., "team familiarity" weighted at 40% for a brand-new team), and for scores with no justification text. A trade-off matrix that produces the "obvious" answer with suspiciously round weights deserves a second look.

See Deliverable 05 for the full event-backbone trade-off matrix built for MedFlow AI, including the weighted-score chart.

The Architect's 20%

Key Patterns Worth Knowing by Name

You don't need to memorize a pattern catalog. You need to recognize these when an AI proposes them, know what problem each one actually solves, and know its cost — because every pattern here is used, by name, in the MedFlow AI deliverables that follow.

Backend-for-Frontend (BFF)

A dedicated backend tailored to one specific client experience, instead of one general-purpose API serving every client. MedFlow AI uses two: the Patient Intake BFF and the Nurse Triage Console BFF — different users, different data shapes, different availability tiers.

Circuit Breaker

Stop calling a dependency that's failing, fast, instead of letting every request hang until it times out. Used at the Symptom NLP Orchestrator's boundary with Azure OpenAI — see the failure-handling notes in Deliverable 01.

Event Bus / Pub-Sub

Producers publish facts; consumers subscribe independently. Decouples the Triage Scoring Service from needing to know who cares about a score being produced. The spine of MedFlow AI's Container diagram.

Idempotency Key

A unique key attached to an operation so replaying it (retry, duplicate event delivery) produces the same end state instead of a duplicate side effect. Applied to every HL7 write-back in MedFlow AI.

Anti-Corruption Layer

A translation boundary that keeps a legacy system's data model from leaking into your new system's domain model. The HL7 Interface Engine plays this role between MedFlow AI and MeridianChart.

Strangler Fig

Incrementally replace a legacy capability by routing traffic to the new implementation piece by piece, rather than a big-bang rewrite. Not used in Phase 1 of MedFlow AI (there's nothing legacy being replaced yet) but the pattern to reach for if MeridianChart's triage module is ever retired in favor of MedFlow AI directly.

The Architect's 20%

Fitness Functions

A fitness function is an automated, objective check that a specific architectural characteristic still holds — run continuously, in CI, the same way a unit test protects a function's behavior. It turns "the architecture should stay decoupled" from a hope into something that fails a build.

Architectural characteristicFitness function example (MedFlow AI)
Service boundary integrity (no illegal coupling)Dependency-analysis check in CI that fails the build if the Triage Scoring Service imports anything from the Care Routing Service's internal package — cross-service calls must go through the event bus or a published API contract.
Latency budget (NFR-PERF-02)Synthetic transaction in staging that fails the pipeline if p95 intake-to-score latency exceeds 3.0s under a fixed load profile.
PHI never reaches the LLM identifiableContract test on the Symptom NLP Orchestrator's outbound payload asserting zero matches against a PHI-pattern detector (name, MRN, DOB) before any call to Azure OpenAI is allowed to fire.
No autonomous clinical decisioning (ADR-002 boundary)A static check over the Triage Scoring Service's API contract that fails the build if any response schema field is renamed from *_recommendation to anything implying a final decision (e.g. diagnosis, final_acuity without an acknowledged_by field).
Why this matters for AI-directed engineering specifically

Fitness functions are what let you approve an architecture once and then trust that future AI-generated changes won't silently violate it. Without them, every future change requires you to re-review the whole system by eye. With them, the CI pipeline does that re-review for you, every time.

The Architect's 20%

Build vs. Buy

The default should be buy. Building is justified only when the capability is genuinely core to your competitive differentiation, or nothing on the market meets a hard constraint (regulatory, data-residency, or otherwise).

AskLeans BuyLeans Build
Is this capability core to why customers choose us?No — it's infrastructure everyone needsYes — it's the actual product
Does a vendor solution meet our compliance/data-residency constraints?YesNo usable vendor exists
What's the total cost including integration and ongoing ops?Lower than build + maintainLower than license + integrate at our scale
How fast do we need it?NowWe have runway to build it right

Applied: the MedFlow AI symptom-intake NLP layer

Meridian already holds an enterprise Azure agreement including a contracted, HIPAA-eligible Azure OpenAI Service commitment with a no-data-retention/no-training clause. Building a proprietary clinical NLP model from scratch would take a specialized ML team Meridian doesn't have, months of clinical-data-scarce training, and ongoing model-safety validation — for a capability (natural-language symptom structuring) that is not Meridian's competitive differentiator; the differentiator is the workflow around it. Decision: buy the LLM capability, build a thin proprietary orchestration and safety layer around it. Full reasoning in ADR-006.

Good vs. Bad Architecture

Neither extreme is about complexity. A good architecture matches its complexity to the problem; a bad one mismatches it — in either direction.

Good architectureBad architecture
Every quality attribute has a measurable target tied to a business reason.NFRs are vague ("fast," "secure") or absent.
Complexity matches the actual load and team topology.Microservices for a six-person internal tool, or a monolith for a 400K-request-per-second system.
Every significant decision has an ADR with rejected alternatives.Decisions exist only in the code, undocumented and unreviewable.
Failure modes are designed for before they happen (timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, dead-letter handling).Happy-path only; failure handling is "we'll add it later."
Trade-offs are visible: every choice states what it costs, not just what it gains.Decisions are presented as pure wins with no downside acknowledged.
The system can be explained to a new engineer using the C4 model in under 30 minutes.Understanding the system requires reading the entire codebase.

The Architect's Review Lens

This is the checklist to run over any AI-generated technical design before you approve it. Every deliverable in this guide was built to pass it.

1. Are the quality attributes measurable?

Every NFR has a number and a verification method — not an adjective.

2. Is the architecture style justified by load and team shape?

Not chosen by default, not over-engineered, not under-engineered.

3. Does every significant decision have an ADR with rejected alternatives?

If the only option discussed is the one chosen, the decision wasn't actually evaluated.

4. Do trade-offs state costs, not just wins?

A trade-off matrix or ADR with zero downsides listed is incomplete, not clean.

5. Is the failure path designed, not assumed?

Timeouts, retries, circuit breakers, and a defined recovery path exist at every external boundary.

6. Does the design cross any governance boundary silently?

New vendor, compliance posture, or production infrastructure change hiding inside an "implementation detail."

7. Can a junior engineer understand it from the C4 diagrams alone?

If the diagrams need the author standing next to them to explain it, it's not documented — it's illustrated.

8. Is it no bigger than the problem requires?

Every additional service, queue, or abstraction should trace back to a specific quality attribute it satisfies.

This is the lens applied to every deliverable that follows

The eight documents in the rest of this guide were authored to survive exactly this checklist — including stating rejected alternatives, listing costs alongside benefits, and designing failure paths explicitly. Use this checklist on your own future projects the same way.

1. Introduction & Purpose

This document defines the target-state architecture for MedFlow AI, a clinical decision-support platform for patient intake, triage-acuity recommendation, and care-team routing at Meridian Health Network. It is the primary reference for engineering, security, clinical informatics, and audit stakeholders, and the artifact against which all subsequent design changes are evaluated. Business requirements are assumed approved by the Clinical Operations Steering Committee (2026-06-30) and are not re-litigated here.

2. Scope

In-scope for Phase 1 (General Availability): conversational patient intake (English/Spanish), hybrid rules+ML triage acuity recommendation, care-team queue routing, and HL7v2/FHIR write-back to MeridianChart across all 12 Meridian facilities. Explicitly out of scope: autonomous triage decisioning, cross-facility capacity load-balancing, and additional languages beyond the GA pair — see the Case Study section of this guide for the full scope table.

4. Architectural Goals & Constraints

Goals

  • Reduce average triage wait time by 30% within two flu seasons of GA.
  • Keep the triage-critical path at 99.95% availability.
  • Never let the system render an autonomous clinical decision.

Constraints

  • Must integrate with MeridianChart via existing HL7v2/FHIR interfaces — no direct database access.
  • Must run on the existing Azure Enterprise Agreement.
  • Must not trigger FDA Software-as-a-Medical-Device (SaMD) reclassification.

5. System Context

MedFlow AI sits between three human actor groups (Patient, Triage Nurse, Care Coordinator) and four external systems (MeridianChart EHR, Azure OpenAI Service, Twilio, Okta). The full annotated Context diagram is in Deliverable 02.

6. Architecture Overview & Style Justification

MedFlow AI is an event-driven microservices architecture, hosted on Azure. This style was chosen — not defaulted to — because three quality attributes specifically require it: differentiated availability tiers between the Patient Intake BFF and the triage-critical path, multiple independent consumers of the same domain events (scoring, audit, future analytics), and independent deployability across teams with different release cadences. Full reasoning and rejected alternatives are in ADR-001.

7. Container-Level Design

Ten containers make up the system: two BFFs (Patient Intake, Nurse Triage Console), three domain services (Symptom NLP Orchestrator, Triage Scoring Service, Care Routing Service), an event bus (Azure Event Hubs), a hybrid HL7 Interface Engine (Mirth Connect), an Audit & Compliance Service, an operational Postgres database, and a Redis session cache. Full container diagram, responsibilities, and communication protocols are in Deliverable 02.

8. Data Architecture

Operational data (patient sessions, intake responses, triage scores, routing decisions, audit events) is persisted in Azure Database for PostgreSQL, partitioned by facility, with all PHI-bearing columns encrypted at rest (AES-256). Session-scoped intermediate state lives in Azure Cache for Redis with a 4-hour TTL. Patient identity is never sent to Azure OpenAI in identifiable form — see the de-identification boundary in Deliverable 06. The full entity model is in the ERD in Deliverable 06.

9. Integration Architecture

All communication with MeridianChart is mediated through a Mirth Connect HL7 Interface Engine, deployed hybrid (on Meridian's private network segment, adjacent to the existing EHR interface tier), acting as an anti-corruption layer so MeridianChart's HL7v2 segment structure never leaks into MedFlow AI's domain model. Full reasoning in ADR-003. Inbound patient identity/demographics arrive via the existing ADT feed; triage outcomes are written back as HL7v2 ORU messages and, where supported, FHIR R4 Encounter/Observation resources.

10. Security Architecture

  • Authentication: Okta SSO + MFA for all clinical users; session timeout 15 minutes idle (NFR-SEC-03).
  • Encryption: TLS 1.2+ on every hop, AES-256 at rest for all PHI-bearing stores (NFR-SEC-01/02).
  • De-identification boundary: the Symptom NLP Orchestrator strips patient-identifying fields before any call to Azure OpenAI and re-associates the response using a session-scoped token map that never leaves Meridian's private network.
  • Audit: every PHI read/write is logged with actor, timestamp, patient reference, and correlation ID, retained 7 years (NFR-COMP-01).

11. Deployment / Infrastructure View

Azure hosts the Patient Intake BFF, Nurse Triage Console BFF, Symptom NLP Orchestrator, Triage Scoring Service, Care Routing Service, Event Hubs namespace, Audit & Compliance Service, PostgreSQL, and Redis — all within an Azure landing zone under the existing Meridian Enterprise Agreement, with a paired-region DR posture (NFR-REL-01/02). The Mirth Connect HL7 Interface Engine remains hybrid, deployed on Meridian's private network adjacent to MeridianChart, connected to the Azure environment via a private ExpressRoute circuit rather than public internet. Full reasoning in ADR-004.

12. Cross-Cutting Concerns

12.1 Observability

Every request on the critical path (intake → scoring → routing) carries a correlation ID propagated through HTTP headers, event payloads, and log lines, satisfying NFR-OBS-01. Structured JSON logs ship to Azure Monitor; dashboards track the KPIs defined in the Architecture KPIs section of this guide.

12.2 Failure Handling

Designed failure path: Azure OpenAI unavailable

The Symptom NLP Orchestrator wraps its Azure OpenAI calls in a circuit breaker (trip after 3 consecutive failures or a 10s timeout). On trip, the Patient Intake BFF falls back to a structured multiple-choice intake form — no free-text NLP, but the patient can still complete intake and reach a triage nurse. An alert pages the on-call engineer; the breaker attempts a half-open retry every 60 seconds. Intake is never fully blocked by an LLM outage.

12.3 Idempotency

Every HL7 write-back to MeridianChart carries an idempotency key of (session_id, event_type); the HL7 Interface Engine checks for a prior successful write before re-sending on retry, so a duplicate delivery from the event bus can never produce a duplicate chart entry.

13. NFR Summary

The full, measurable Non-Functional Requirements Specification is Deliverable 04. Headline targets: p95 intake response <2.0s, p95 triage score delivery <3.0s, 99.95% critical-path availability, RPO ≤5 min / RTO ≤30 min.

14. Risk Summary

The full Architecture Risk Register is Deliverable 07. Top risk: automation bias — a triage nurse over-trusting the AI recommendation without independent clinical judgment (R-01, Critical severity).

ADR-001 — Event-Driven Microservices over a Monolithic Extension

Accepted

Context

MeridianChart's vendor customization layer could technically host triage-assist logic as a plugin. Meridian must satisfy two different availability tiers (99.95% critical path vs. 99.9% intake) and give the Audit Service, Triage Scoring Service, and future analytics independent, non-blocking access to the same intake-completion fact.

Decision

Build MedFlow AI as a standalone event-driven microservices platform integrated with MeridianChart via HL7v2/FHIR, not as a plugin inside the EHR's customization layer.

Rejected Alternatives

  • EHR vendor customization plugin. Rejected: shares the EHR's single release train and availability envelope, making a differentiated 99.95%/99.9% SLA split impossible; vendor certification cycle for plugin changes is 6-8 weeks, incompatible with iterative delivery.
  • Single new monolith service. Rejected: works initially, but collapses the moment a second independent consumer (Audit Service) needs the same event — every new consumer would require a code change inside the monolith rather than an independent subscription.

Consequences

Gain: independent scaling and deployment per service; clean audit trail via event replay. Cost: operational complexity of running and monitoring 6+ independently deployable services instead of 1; eventual consistency between intake completion and score availability must be explicitly designed for (see NFR-PERF-02).

ADR-002 — Hybrid Rules + ML Ensemble for Triage Acuity, Not Pure ML

Accepted

Context

A pure ML model could plausibly produce more accurate acuity predictions on average. But an autonomous or purely ML-driven clinical recommendation risks reclassification as an FDA Software-as-a-Medical-Device (SaMD), which would trigger a multi-year regulatory pathway Meridian's program is not funded or scoped for.

Decision

Triage acuity is computed by a deterministic, auditable Rules Engine (ESI-based) running alongside an ML Inference Adapter; a Safety Arbiter combines both under the fixed policy "rules may only escalate acuity, never downgrade it," and the combined output is always labeled a recommendation requiring explicit nurse acknowledgment before it affects the chart.

Rejected Alternatives

  • Pure ML model, autonomous output. Rejected: crosses into FDA CDS-exemption-losing territory per 21st Century Cures Act guidance on the boundary between decision support and autonomous decisioning; unacceptable regulatory risk.
  • Pure rules engine, no ML. Rejected: forgoes the accuracy and nuance gains of an ML signal entirely; clinically weaker without a commensurate safety gain, since the arbiter policy already bounds ML's ability to under-triage.

Consequences

Gain: stays inside the FDA's clinical decision-support exemption; deterministic rules component is fully explainable to auditors and clinicians. Cost: more complex scoring pipeline (two engines + an arbiter) than either approach alone; ML component's incremental accuracy benefit is capped by the "escalate-only" safety policy.

ADR-003 — HL7v2/FHIR Interface Engine over Direct Database Integration

Accepted

Context

MedFlow AI needs patient demographics on intake and must write triage outcomes back into MeridianChart. A direct database connection to MeridianChart's schema would be technically faster to build.

Decision

All integration with MeridianChart goes through a Mirth Connect HL7 Interface Engine using the existing ADT feed (inbound) and HL7v2 ORU / FHIR R4 Encounter-Observation resources (outbound) — never a direct database connection.

Rejected Alternatives

  • Direct database read/write against MeridianChart's schema. Rejected: couples MedFlow AI to the EHR vendor's internal schema, which changes on the vendor's upgrade cycle without notice; violates the EHR vendor's support agreement; bypasses the EHR's own data-integrity and audit hooks.
  • Vendor-proprietary API-only integration (no HL7 engine). Rejected: the vendor's REST API does not yet cover triage-outcome write-back for Meridian's contracted module tier; would require a separate paid module upgrade not currently budgeted.

Consequences

Gain: integration is vendor-upgrade-safe and stays within the supported interface surface; the interface engine acts as an anti-corruption layer, isolating MedFlow AI's domain model from EHR schema churn. Cost: HL7v2 message construction and parsing is more verbose than a direct API/DB call; the interface engine itself becomes a component that must be operated and monitored (see Risk R-02).

ADR-004 — Azure (Hybrid) over AWS or Full On-Premise

Accepted

Context

Meridian already holds an enterprise agreement with Microsoft covering Azure consumption and a contracted, HIPAA-eligible Azure OpenAI Service commitment with no data-retention/training clause. Meridian's data center is mid-migration to Azure for other workloads.

Decision

Host MedFlow AI's cloud-native containers on Azure, keeping the HL7 Interface Engine hybrid (on Meridian's private network, connected via ExpressRoute) rather than fully on-premise or on a second cloud vendor.

Rejected Alternatives

  • AWS. Rejected: would require a net-new BAA and enterprise agreement negotiation, duplicate identity federation work with Okta, and cross-cloud egress cost/latency for no offsetting capability gain — Azure OpenAI already satisfies the LLM requirement under the existing agreement.
  • Full on-premise. Rejected: cannot meet the 800-concurrent-session flu-season burst target (NFR-SCALE-01) without over-provisioning idle on-prem capacity for 10 months of the year; conflicts with Meridian's active on-prem-to-Azure migration direction.

Consequences

Gain: reuses existing enterprise agreement, identity federation, and network connectivity; autoscale absorbs seasonal peak load without idle capacity cost. Cost: the HL7 Interface Engine's hybrid placement adds a network dependency (ExpressRoute) whose outage becomes a shared point of failure between MedFlow AI and other Azure-hosted Meridian workloads.

ADR-005 — Azure Event Hubs as the Cross-Service Event Backbone

Accepted

Context

Intake completion, triage scoring, and routing decisions must fan out to multiple independent consumers (scoring, audit, future analytics) without tight coupling. See the full weighted trade-off analysis in Deliverable 05.

Decision

Use Azure Event Hubs (Kafka-protocol compatible) as the event backbone, topic-partitioned by facility.

Rejected Alternatives

  • Confluent Cloud (managed Kafka). Rejected: strongest latency/throughput profile of the options evaluated, but requires PHI-adjacent event metadata to cross a cloud boundary out of the Azure compliance envelope, needing a second BAA; scored 3.55/5 weighted vs. Event Hubs' 4.25/5.
  • Self-hosted RabbitMQ on AKS. Rejected: cheapest option and no vendor lock-in, but shifts cluster operation, patching, and HA configuration onto a platform team not sized for it; scored 3.65/5 weighted.
  • AWS SQS/SNS. Rejected: would duplicate ADR-004's cross-cloud objections at the messaging layer specifically; scored 2.75/5 weighted, lowest of the four.

Consequences

Gain: stays inside the existing Azure compliance boundary and BAA; Kafka-protocol compatibility keeps a future migration path open if requirements outgrow Event Hubs. Cost: Event Hubs' consumer-group model is less flexible than self-hosted Kafka for complex routing topologies; throughput-unit-based pricing requires active capacity planning ahead of flu-season peaks (NFR-SCALE-02).

ADR-006 — Buy the LLM Capability, Build the Orchestration Layer

Accepted

Context

Symptom-intake conversational NLP could be built as a proprietary clinical language model or bought as a managed service. See the Build vs. Buy framework applied in full in the discipline section of this guide.

Decision

License Azure OpenAI Service (already under contract, HIPAA-eligible, no-retention/no-training terms) for the underlying LLM capability; build a proprietary Symptom NLP Orchestrator that owns de-identification, prompt construction, response structuring, and the provider-abstraction interface in-house.

Rejected Alternatives

  • Build a proprietary clinical NLP model from scratch. Rejected: requires a specialized ML research team Meridian does not have, months of clinically-scarce labeled training data, and independent model-safety validation, for a capability that is not Meridian's competitive differentiator.
  • Buy a fully packaged third-party clinical-intake SaaS product (LLM + UI + workflow). Rejected: would replace, not integrate with, Meridian's existing HL7 interface investment and Okta identity federation, and locks the entire patient-facing experience — not just the LLM call — to one vendor's roadmap.

Consequences

Gain: fastest path to a clinically safe intake experience; the internal orchestration layer keeps the AI provider swappable behind one interface, limiting lock-in to the LLM call itself rather than the whole workflow. Cost: ongoing dependency on Azure OpenAI Service pricing and availability (see Risk R-03, R-09); the orchestration layer is Meridian-maintained code that must absorb any future provider API changes.
